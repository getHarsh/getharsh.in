# From the plant to the team

At Sant Parmanand Hospital in New Delhi, I could read the chiller over MODBUS and plot what it was doing. That felt like a good beginning. Arjun, [Smart Joules](https://smartjoules.co.in/)' CEO, and I had obtained time and permission to experiment with the HVAC system. The operators, meanwhile, ran the plant through the red and green buttons familiar to them. We were looking at the same equipment through rather different accounts. My chart made a part of the operation visible, but it had not yet given the operator a reason to need it.

I joined in November 2016 as the founding engineer, working in embedded systems. A reading arriving was an achievement, but it opened another question: what did the reading let somebody understand? The operator did not encounter a collection of protocols. They encountered equipment that served a building and a problem they needed to locate. The product had to connect the engineering account to that situation.

Measurement had already been part of my thesis work, Project Netra. I served as its Lead Researcher with NEFORD and [ICAR-CSSRI](https://cssri.res.in/lucknow-rrs/)'s Regional Research Station in Lucknow from December 2015 to September 2016, then continued as a volunteer and mentor through September 2020. Working with images and embedded measurements made the path from an input to its interpretation interesting to me. Choosing a reference, selecting a region or converting a reading changes what the returned number can mean. At the plant, that path acquired another consequence: somebody needed to use it to keep equipment working.

<a id="what-does-a-blank-reading-mean-to-the-operator"></a>

## What does a blank reading mean to the operator?

A missing value can come from the instrument, its mapping, a controller or a connection further along the path. To the person looking at the screen, it may appear simply as an absent part of the plant. Exposing the technical error is useful to an engineer; locating its consequence is useful to an operator. A diagnostic view has to make the connection between them.

That changed how I thought about an interface. Its job was not to present every fact the system possessed. It needed to preserve the distinctions that would change a person's next move. Was the equipment off? Was its reading unavailable? Had a command been sent without [its physical result being confirmed](https://getharsh.in/collections/energy-efficiency/from-service-to-control/did-the-command-reach-the-pump/)? The answers could look similar on a chart and demand different responses in the plant.

The turning point for me was that product judgment was already inside the engineering. Choosing what to measure, how to explain a gap and which consequence to follow was choosing what kind of help the system could offer.

<a id="three-months-building-what-we-already-had"></a>

## Three months building what we already had

My responsibilities expanded into architecture, product direction, hiring and product-development leadership. I recruited seven initial direct reports and, through them, grew a development team of twenty-five across research, hardware, software, testing, production and design. During that period, delivery and operations, which I partly managed, grew to eighty-four people, and the company to 115. The development disciplines needed to meet around the same outcome. A component could be locally correct and still be difficult for another team to integrate or for the field team to maintain. Splitting the work into departments did not by itself carry its meaning across the split.

One coordination failure stayed with me: work continued for nearly three months on a duplicate service before the existing one was discovered. The difficulty was not a lack of people willing to build. It was a lack of shared context about what already existed and why another team might need it. A roadmap could name the deliverable without making that relationship visible.

I became more interested in the explanation around the task. What problem does this part solve? Which other work does it depend on? What will another person need when they receive it? A brief, review or conversation is valuable when it carries those answers far enough that people can make decisions without repeatedly returning to the person who remembers everything.

I still meet that difficulty when [returning to my own work](https://getharsh.in/collections/building-products-and-teams/building-a-place-to-think/). The remembered answer can be clear while the grounds for it have disappeared. Shared context has to make the next decision easier, not merely preserve the conversation that once made it easy.

<a id="making-room-for-somebody-new"></a>

## Making room for somebody new

[JouleLabs](https://smartjoules.co.in/joulehub/joulelabs) came from a related possibility: students and mentors could work on [actual product problems](https://getharsh.in/collections/learning-and-practice/a-real-problem-is-a-better-learning-brief/) while developing the capability to contribute. I founded and ran the programme alongside the development work. A real problem gave learning a purpose, but the purpose did not remove the need for a continuing mentor, a review or a team able to receive the result.

Teaching also required me to make my own understanding more explicit. Something that felt obvious after months in the plant was not an adequate starting point for a new contributor. Explaining the setting, allowing a question and seeing where the explanation failed could improve the team's context as well as the learner's.

The work reached more than forty facilities in ten cities, with reported client energy savings above 30%. I represented Smart Joules at Shell’s Make the Future in Singapore, where the company placed third, and spoke on the panel at NREDCAP’s Powering Andhra Pradesh summit. Smart Joules received an award from Piyush Goyal for the product’s effect on energy waste. Those results belonged to the company and the people delivering and operating the service; my responsibility was to help make the product and engineering capable of supporting it.

By the time I left in March 2020, the responsibility had changed. I was still concerned with whether a technical path worked, but also with whether the people around it could understand, extend and sustain it. That question travelled with me. At [Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/), it would begin from the other end: the customer could need less of the system, rather than a better explanation of all that it could do.
