# The customer changes the system

After [Smart Joules](https://smartjoules.co.in/), I was exploring different ways to work. Jobs, advice, partnerships and ventures overlapped; I was not yet moving through a settled sequence towards the practice I have today. Two settings from that period made the product question more demanding. At [Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/), the customer needed a simpler route through a broad suite. At Sachait, a detected event needed an entire response around it. One problem asked me to remove complexity; the other asked me to understand the work a component had left out.

<a id="the-customer-who-needed-less-software"></a>

## The customer who needed less software

I was the first Product Manager hired into Zenoti’s Small Business Unit, working there from December 2020 to March 2022 across support, onboarding and taxation for small businesses in the US and Canada. Support data, product analytics, surveys and conversations gave me different ways into the customer's experience. A ticket could show where someone was stuck; talking to them could reveal what they had been trying to accomplish before the product made them stop.

The suite contained capabilities useful to a much broader set of businesses. Carrying all of them into the small-business experience asked the customer to absorb decisions their own operation did not require. The roadmap therefore changed towards fit: retain the work that mattered and reduce the configuration, navigation and translation around it. The revised experience reached customers in under a year, including self-onboarding and streamlined taxation work. Buddy, the AI-first design system, brought analytics and assistance into that experience. Customer satisfaction rose by 35% across that work.

This altered what I considered an ambitious product decision. [Removing a feature](https://getharsh.in/blog/making-a-product-smaller/) can require a deeper understanding of the customer than adding one. The team has to know which task remains possible, which exception still needs a path and where an apparently convenient default would hide a consequential choice. Technical fluency helps explain the dependency; product judgment decides how much of it the customer should have to manage.

<a id="then-there-was-the-person-responding-to-an-emergency"></a>

## Then there was the person responding to an emergency

From December 2020 to July 2022, I worked with the Sachait team, first as an adviser and then as a cofounder leading product and engineering. The work continued alongside Zenoti. The company had been Sachait Elecbits; during my involvement, the Elecbits partnership ended and it was formally renamed Sachait Technologies Pvt. Ltd. I joined an existing service and worked with its operations data, neighbourhood watch, the 112 authorities and pilot customers in the Lucknow–Kanpur region to build a technology-enabled emergency-response workflow.

Here, making the detector better was only one part of the product. A person needed enough context to judge an event, another person might need to continue from that judgment, and the situation could change while the system was passing it along. The result was experienced as a service, not as the output of an isolated model. Interface, evidence and handoff decisions shaped what the model's result could become.

The work covered more than 7,500 emergency scenarios and a model with more than 7,500 parameters; those are separate counts. The reported results included 85% fewer false positives and pilot response in under five minutes. The service reached ₹4 lakh in monthly recurring revenue with 90% retention. A detector’s improvement and a service’s continued use were different kinds of evidence, and both mattered to whether the work could continue.

That setting gave a different meaning to simplicity. Fewer steps can help somebody act under stress, but omitting a necessary distinction can make the next decision harder. An operator needs to distinguish an event they have reviewed from one whose evidence they could not obtain. The people coordinating a response need to know what remains unresolved, not only that a notification was sent. I exited through a patent acquisition, with safe returns for investors and cofounders, and the work continued as [YOJN Care](https://daostudio.in/yojn-care/) (on DAO Studio) at DAO Studio. The acquired IP concerned a patent application; the transaction is not a claim that a patent had been granted.

<a id="where-the-useful-product-ends"></a>

## Where the useful product ends

The connection between these experiences is not that the systems were alike. It is that the useful product extended beyond the component or screen I was initially looking at. Onboarding included migration and provider dependencies. Emergency response included evidence, people and continuity. A better interface had to change the burden of the work, rather than merely hide it.

Building ventures added another set of questions. Who would adopt the system, who would support it and what could sustain the service once an enthusiastic first demonstration was over? A set of proposals from 2022 explored how consulting, education and other programmes might reinforce one another. Not all those arrangements endured. [The gap between a useful problem and a sustainable vehicle](https://getharsh.in/collections/building-products-and-teams/what-remains-when-an-experiment-ends/) became a question I had to take seriously.

Product management and engineering became harder to separate in my practice. I needed the mechanism to understand what could change, and the customer's situation to understand what was worth changing. A product decision could ask for less software or for a larger service around it. Learning which situation I was in mattered more than starting with the technology I wanted to build.
