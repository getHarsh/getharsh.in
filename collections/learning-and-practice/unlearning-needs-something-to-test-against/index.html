The discipline after unlearning

It all started with “time”.

I could learn the engineering language for measuring a passage of time without feeling that I had answered what time was. The distinction sounds abstract until it unsettles a familiar explanation: a clock tells us something important, but naming the instrument does not finish the question.

That curiosity led me through technical study, philosophy and traditions I had grown up around. It also made me suspicious of a particular kind of education: the kind that gives us answers so efficiently that we stop noticing which questions those answers leave open.

Reading what I was told not to read

I grew up in Lucknow in a Hindu Brahmin family, with roots in Almora. A custom kept the Katha Upanishad away from ordinary reading, associating it with death. Being the rebel and curious soul I was, I read it anyway. What I found was, in a way, a podcast: a young boy, Nachiketa, in conversation with Yama about death, desire, what endures and what deserves to be pursued. The text became something I could examine for myself, rather than something defined by the inherited restriction around it.

I also wrote about the Nirvana Shatkam and its movement through negation: not this, not that. Reading it as a hymn was one encounter; following its negations as an exercise was another. Take away the identification with a body, a thought, a role or a description. What remains? In my reading, its movement towards Shiva and the bliss of consciousness was a process to go through, rather than a conclusion to repeat. It asked me to notice how quickly I had attached an identity to each thing it was taking away.

Other readings, including the Saundarya Lahari and Vigyan Bhairav, opened further paths. Ancient podcasts and publications, if you will, encountered through dialogue, negation, description and practice. Kaal, MahaKaal and Kaali became part of that inquiry: what were these texts saying about time through those names and images? All because I had been trying to find out what time was.

I began to wonder whether a civilisation organised around these inquiries understood something we have become poor at recognising. I called ours a civilisation of electromagnetism, a species of Maxwell, and imagined those older worlds through legends whose meanings might have been lost in translation. That is an intuition inviting study, not a historical demonstration supplied by the resemblance. What I wanted to recover was a scholarly encounter with the texts: enough freedom to question a custom, and enough patience to discover that the passage was not saying what I expected.

Unlearning, for me, began less as a declaration against a tradition than as a refusal to let the inherited instruction be the last thing said about it.

The comfort of a new allegiance

Once an inherited belief becomes questionable, rebellion can feel like clarity. The emotional reward arrives before the harder work. I can stop trusting an institution and begin trusting whichever voice most strongly opposes it, or adopt an unfamiliar explanation because its novelty feels liberating. Replacing the vocabulary leaves the old habit intact if I still accept conclusions without examining how they were reached. A question opens possibilities; the first alternative deserves examination too.

When I ask what time is, there are several kinds of work available. I can study what established physical theories say, examine how duration is experienced, read a story in which time is imagined differently, or propose a model and ask what observation would distinguish it from another. These inquiries can illuminate one another without supplying interchangeable answers. A compelling experience does not establish a physical mechanism; an equation does not tell me everything about how time is lived. Understanding what each encounter offers makes their connection useful, rather than merely exciting.

Getting very good at the teacher's answer

Education often rewards what can be repeated: a teacher needs some way to judge whether a learner has acquired the material. Yet a person can learn the vocabulary of independent thinking while becoming very skilled at anticipating what the teacher wants to hear. The same difference appears in technical work when a completed tutorial leaves the next unfamiliar condition unnavigable. Acquiring the explanation has value; making it one's own asks for another kind of encounter with the subject.

I am interested in learning that makes the learner more capable of noticing: what is missing from the explanation, which assumption was invisible before, or what they need to observe, build or experience to understand the question better. The result does not have to be certainty. A more precise doubt can give the next act of learning a direction.

The school I was imagining

The question led me to imagine an institution: a place where curiosity could develop through examining one's conditioning, meeting unfamiliar ideas and building something from the encounter. Individual advice can encourage inquiry, but the setting has to make it possible. The institution would need to support a learner through both the inherited answer and the uncertainty that opens when they question it.

That is a much longer project than improving a course outline. A school carries incentives, selection, authority, habits of discussion and an account of what counts as success. It can praise curiosity while punishing the questions that disrupt its own image.

The same scholarly detachment matters in modern research. I see it in open-source systems that let someone else examine the work, and in high-quality peer review that tries to judge an argument without favouring its desired outcome. Chasing a journal’s impact factor can displace that purpose. So can p-hacking: choosing and reworking analyses until the data appear to deliver the statistical result one wanted. An institution cannot teach detachment convincingly while rewarding the appearance of a successful result over an honest inquiry. It has to leave room to question even the concepts on which its own standing rests.

The difficult design problem is how to make inquiry ordinary. Learners need enough structure to become capable, enough freedom to discover a question of their own and enough care that disagreement does not become humiliation. Teachers need room to say they do not know without making every claim equally valid.

I imagine building such a place on a horizon longer than my own lifetime. Centuries of work, perhaps, and no guarantee that those who start it will live to see what it becomes. Detachment from the outcome is therefore part of the institution's design, not merely advice to its students. What would let later people examine its assumptions, rather than preserve the founder's answers? A school devoted to inquiry would eventually have to welcome a question that unsettled its own account of what inquiry was for. Otherwise the next generation would inherit another instruction not to read.

Practical teaching has given the question a nearer form. A real problem, a continuing mentor and work that somebody can use can connect curiosity to consequence. They do not fulfil the entire institutional ambition, but they create a place to begin.

A question that changes what I build

Time led me towards traditions, models and the creative inquiry I now pursue in Sleepwalker. Product work led me towards learning and institutions. Each encounter gave the next question more substance: an unexplained experience could prompt study; an incomplete explanation could prompt a design; teaching could expose an assumption I had stopped noticing.

These encounters keep the world from feeling exhausted by the answers I already have. They give curiosity something to return to, study and build upon. Questioning an explanation opens a space; the patient work that follows lets something richer grow there without making it another allegiance.
