# Learning with a real problem

A tutorial usually knows where it is going. It has chosen the important details, prepared the inputs and arranged a route to completion. A real problem is less courteous. Someone needs something to work; the information is incomplete, the environment resists the example and the person asking for help may not yet know how to describe what is wrong. That difficulty creates a reason to understand, not merely a reason to finish the exercise.

<a id="joulelabs-began-close-to-the-work"></a>

## JouleLabs began close to the work

I founded and ran [JouleLabs](https://smartjoules.co.in/joulehub/joulelabs) while building the product-development team at [Smart Joules](https://smartjoules.co.in/). The question was how someone still learning could make a meaningful contribution to consequential work. A problem statement needed to explain why the work mattered, a mentor needed to continue beyond the initial explanation, and the student's project needed a route through review towards something another person could use. Learning, contribution and possible recruitment could reinforce one another because they met around that shared object. Without the continuity, the student could produce a deliverable and the organisation a certificate, while neither acquired much that changed the next piece of work.

We reached our first university memorandum of understanding in under two months from the idea. The programme eventually involved five universities and a cohort of sixty-five students, with more than forty moving into full-time employment. Those numbers describe the programme’s reach and the transition into work. The educational question was what made that transition possible: a problem worth entering, someone available to review it and a team with a use for the result.

<a id="what-is-the-dashboard-supposed-to-change"></a>

## What is the dashboard supposed to change?

“Build a dashboard” specifies a deliverable. “Help an operator recognise why this measurement cannot yet be trusted” gives the learner a problem to investigate. The second formulation opens questions about the measurement, the operator's task and the decision that follows. It also makes review possible in a more meaningful way: did the person using the result gain something that helps them act? A dashboard might be the answer, but it would now have a reason for its contents.

The problem still needs a boundary. A learner cannot be expected to reconstruct an entire industrial system before making a first contribution. A mentor can isolate a useful question, identify what is safe to examine and provide enough context to prevent the project from becoming arbitrary guesswork. Too much prescription reduces the work to following steps; too little support turns confusion into a test of whether the learner can survive it. The scaffolding earns its place by making the next act of understanding possible, then leaving room for the learner to make it.

<a id="after-the-first-explanation"></a>

## After the first explanation

A real project brings interruptions. The learner finds a missing assumption, the source changes or a first attempt fails. A mentor helps distinguish a useful failure from a misunderstanding that should not consume another week. That means making the problem navigable: where to look, what to compare, how to express the unresolved question and when to ask for help. It takes actual time within the team's work, not just an initial promise that help will be available.

The organisation also needs a way to receive the contribution. If nobody can review or integrate it, the learner's effort ends in a demonstration that has no continuing home. Supporting the programme therefore means reserving capacity on both sides: time for the student to enter the work, and time for the team to examine and use what they bring to it.

<a id="two-hours-a-week-is-a-real-commitment"></a>

## Two hours a week is a real commitment

In my [UPAI](https://upailabs.org/) community participation, time and continuity have been recurring questions. People arrive with different interests, abilities and amounts of unpaid time. A beginner asking for a learning path and an experienced volunteer able to help for two hours a week need different ways into the work. Enthusiasm is valuable, but scheduling it as though it were a permanent team can turn a broad assignment into something nobody can sustain.

A small commitment that someone can finish and another person can rely on can create more continuity. It gives a mentor something concrete to review and a learner a way to experience their contribution becoming useful. Designing participation around the support and time that exist need not exclude those still learning. It can make it easier for them to become capable of work they could not have done alone.

<a id="a-contribution-that-keeps-travelling"></a>

## A contribution that keeps travelling

JouleLabs later inspired Sustainability Mafia’s Climate Ninja programme; I did not run that later programme. The influence interests me because the idea can travel beyond its first institution: practitioner-led learning around live problems gives people a route into useful work. A later programme can develop that route in ways its original setting could not.

This nearer form also connects to the longer ambition in [The discipline after unlearning](https://getharsh.in/collections/learning-and-practice/unlearning-needs-something-to-test-against/). A learner needs more than access to explanations. They need an environment in which a question can become a capability, and a capability can become a contribution.

There is something lovely about a learner discovering that their work has become useful to somebody else. That encounter asks for more care than a certificate does: a problem they can enter, somebody who stays through the confusion and a team willing to receive the result. Participants will want different futures, and some projects will lead somewhere other than integration. The point is to give curiosity enough contact with the world that the person can find their next contribution.
