# The intelligence between the parts

Imagine finding a useful piece of intelligence and putting it beside another. One recognises a situation; the other knows what to do when that situation appears. Connecting them sounds almost embarrassingly straightforward. Recognition goes in, action comes out. But what exactly passed between them? A label, a history, a degree of uncertainty, the thing one of them noticed and the other would otherwise miss? The connection may be carrying more of the intelligence than the diagram admits.

This is a question I have been following through different kinds of work. At [DAO Studio](https://daostudio.in/collections/composing-ai-work/from-composition-to-a-deliberate-change/agent-playbook-and-application/), the operating-system image helped me think about applications, agents and playbooks. A capability could have an interface; a sequence could retain what an earlier step had found; a later step could resume when the missing information arrived. Those were useful ways to arrange work. Underneath them was another ambition that arranging calls could not satisfy: could learned capacities themselves become composable, and could their organisation change what the whole system was able to do?

<a id="the-missing-part-of-the-handoff"></a>

## The missing part of the handoff

Suppose one component reads a passage and reports that a person promised to return. Another selects the next action. The report may be accurate and still be too small. Was the promise conditional? Did it concern tomorrow or a date that has already passed? Was it spoken sincerely, quoted from someone else or inferred by the reader? If the second component needs one of those distinctions, an impeccably delivered label cannot supply it. The apparent quality of the first component conceals an unanswered question about what the two can share.

An application can often solve this through an explicit representation. Carry the relevant fields, retain the source, let the next step ask for what it needs. The agent and playbook work gave that approach a concrete home. I still find it valuable: a visible sequence can expose the point at which interpretation, missing information or a person's decision matters. Yet the more interesting research possibility begins when I cannot specify the whole useful representation in advance. What would let two learned components discover a productive exchange rather than merely conform to the exchange I designed for them?

That question changes the object I am trying to build. A pipeline can work because its author anticipated the intermediate states. A population of learned units would have to make useful states available through its own limited interaction. It might discover a better division of labour, or spend all its time communicating. Giving each unit a name and drawing arrows between them tells me very little about which of those happened.

<a id="a-capacity-has-a-setting"></a>

## A capacity has a setting

The idea of [reusable intelligence](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/the-red-pill/) is attractive partly because software reuse makes it feel familiar. A library function can arrive with a declared input, output and contract. A learned capacity also arrives with a history: examples, surrounding representations, assumptions about the other parts and the resources under which it worked. I cannot treat that history as incidental packaging if changing it changes the capacity.

Consider moving a summariser from a short exchange to a long negotiation. It may still return a fluent account, but the important distinction might now be which proposals were accepted, which were withdrawn and which party could make them. “It summarises” is a poor description of what I need to reuse. A richer account asks what the capacity preserves, when it fails and which downstream decisions remain possible. This is the pressure behind [keeping context inside a reusable capability](https://daostudio.in/collections/protocols-and-reuse/some-things-never-change/): the useful unit is larger than the attractive output.

I work on the practical side of that question through [Pale](https://rawthoughts.in/pale/): make a person-described world operational, with relationships and capabilities the system can already inspect. [Counsel](https://rawthoughts.in/counsel/) supplies local semantic judgment inside that arrangement. The deeper architectural question is how much of the useful organisation could itself be learned, instead of requiring me to specify every exchange.

My research in [Causality](https://causality.in/about/) separates the question of one unit from the question of many. [MUON](https://causality.in/muon/) asks what a small adaptive unit might be; [HENA](https://causality.in/hena/) asks what organisation among units might make possible. The relationship between Counsel and MUON, and between Pale and HENA, gives me two scales at which to follow the ambition. A better unit could change the judgment within an arrangement; a better many-unit architecture could change how the arrangement itself reasons and organises. The surrounding experience could become simpler as the intelligence beneath it learns more of that work. That separation gives the ambition something to work against. If I change a unit and the whole improves, did the unit improve, did its communication become easier, or did the arrangement give it more resources? I want to understand the contribution, because the answer determines what is reusable.

There are narrower ways into the compatibility question. [Git Re-Basin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.04836) studies aligning hidden units through permutations before merging independently trained models in weight space. It gives me something more precise than the wish to move a useful piece of intelligence: what transformation would make two representations compatible, and what behaviour should survive the combination? That is not yet the same as locating a separable capacity to transfer, but it makes one part of the difficulty available for investigation.

<a id="memory-makes-the-arrangement-matter"></a>

## Memory makes the arrangement matter

Imagine a clue arriving early, before anyone knows why it is important. Much later, another part receives a request for which that clue would be decisive. The first part can keep the clue, but keeping it privately does not help the second. Broadcasting everything would make it available and defeat the point of a limited system. A useful arrangement has to make some combination of retention, routing and later recognition work together.

This is why distributed memory interests me more than a large bag of messages. The arrangement decides what can become relevant across time. A small unit may have enough capability for its local task while the population fails because nobody can find what another unit kept. Conversely, a simple unit can become useful through a well-placed connection. The [early-clue construction](https://causality.in/collections/composition-and-learning/from-unit-to-swarm/emergence-needs-a-comparison/) makes that problem small enough to examine: local competence and collective usefulness are different things we can actually compare.

There is a personal attraction here too. I work across fields in which something encountered casually can become useful much later. The temptation is to call that cross-pollination and leave its operation mysterious. Research lets me ask a narrower question without losing the attraction: what survived the interval, what made it accessible and what did the later work gain from it? A system that simply repeats more of its past has not necessarily become more capable of making those connections.

The operating-system image remains useful to me, but it now opens onto several different problems. There is the arrangement of work that I can describe explicitly. There is the learned capacity that I would like to understand well enough to move. There is the organisation that might make partial capacities useful together. I do not need one familiar metaphor to finish all three. What keeps me following them is the possibility that intelligence can live partly in how an encounter is carried forward, not only in the component that first understood it.
