A future that remembers

Meaning, models and creative practice

Imagine a room with a terminal, a lamp, books and a cup somebody has left beside their work. Beyond the window, an immense machine hangs in the distance. Nobody in the room treats it as a spectacle. The lamp still has to light the desk; the person still has a page to finish. I find this future more interesting than one in which every ordinary object has disappeared into an immaculate wall.

What draws me to it is the possibility of technological change without cultural amnesia. An inherited form need not be a relic, and an advanced instrument need not look as though it arrived from outside the life around it. A civilisation can maintain its objects, rituals and places while discovering new things they can do. The future I want to imagine has had time to become familiar.

A machine with a history

A perfectly seamless device invites me to treat it as a finished surface. A housing, a switch, an instrument panel or a visible connection gives it a body. Someone may have opened it, changed a part, understood its behaviour well enough to keep it running. Those possibilities alter the world implied by the image. The object belongs to a history of use rather than merely a catalogue of the latest technology.

That is part of my attraction to analog technological futures. Rounded screens, deep bezels, lamps and physical controls are forms I can imagine being inhabited, maintained and passed on. Their significance is not that old hardware was universally better. It is that technology occupies a place in human life. When everything becomes a frictionless pane, I can lose the sense of how a person relates to what sustains them.

The same thought changes how I imagine a sacred or civic building. A temple containing an advanced instrument need not be a temple with a futuristic accessory pasted onto it. What if its material, spatial customs, maintenance and observation had developed together? A shrine and a terminal room could come from the same technological tradition. I want to discover how that continuity would feel in a world, rather than make a historical claim by drawing the two beside each other.

Recognition, followed by surprise

There is a particular encounter I keep trying to design: an object first appears familiar, then turns out to be doing something I did not expect. At rest, it has the discipline of an artifact. In use, a held state, a movement or a change of light reveals that it is alive as a system. Recognition gives the surprise somewhere to land. Without it, the experience can become another sequence of impressive effects with nothing to hold onto.

That ambition was behind my earlier studies of programmable geometry, interfaces and expressive physical objects. I wanted an identity that could move between a screen, a light and a piece of sound without becoming a logo repeatedly applied to different surfaces. The more difficult question was when that range should appear. If everything moves, movement stops telling me anything. If the system is so restrained that it never reveals its character, the restraint has become a loss of possibility.

The dragon drainpipe is a useful image for what I mean. It carries water away and still has a face, a story, a little delight. Its poetry has not excused it from doing the ordinary job. Its ordinary job has not required it to abandon the poetry. I want technology to hold that conjunction more often: something exact enough to trust and alive enough to care about.

A quiet frame for a vivid world

The frame around an experience can demand attention that the experience needed for itself. A reader may arrive to follow an argument and spend their first moments learning a new navigation ritual. An image may be crowded by surrounding effects, each announcing that the interface is special. I want the machinery to spend that attention carefully. A clear, addressable interaction can remain quiet until it is called, then respond directly.

The terminal interests me in this sense: naming, querying, changing a state and seeing a response. I do not need a screen full of fake prompts to preserve that manner. In a publication, the reading surface can carry stable habits while the world around the work changes through material, geometry, type and light. The reader should not have to relearn how to move every time the atmosphere becomes different.

An image then has room to carry more of the expressive burden. A cool field and one warm source can establish a relation before detailed textures establish materials. Overlap, distance and a silhouette can make a place dimensional without making every surface equally explicit. This is why I care about the composition of a human witness within a larger world. What matters first is where the person stands in relation to the light, the opening, the thing that exceeds them. An inventory of beautiful surfaces can leave that relation strangely absent.

Ordinary life gives the immense world weight

A monumental image can make a person useful only as a ruler. A small figure demonstrates that the structure is large, then has no more work to do. I want the figure to carry something of a life. A desk, a return path, a recognisable object or a lit window can suggest that the place has uses beyond the moment we are watching. The extraordinary becomes more convincing when the world can afford to treat some of it as ordinary.

That conviction reaches into how I think about an actual working space. Books, music, research and making need room alongside the rest of a household's life. The design should absorb difficult services and connections so the room can remain open, usable and quiet. I do not want every technical possibility to become something its inhabitants must constantly operate. Nor do I want the quietness to depend on pretending the systems underneath it do not exist.

A future acquires depth when some things persist through its transformations. In a sequence, a remembered object or a stable threshold can carry that persistence even as the surrounding register changes. In a technological world, an inherited custom or repairable instrument can do similar imaginative work. It gives the new thing a relation to what was already there.

I want a future with enough memory that people can belong to it, and enough possibility that belonging does not become a demand to leave everything unchanged. The books and the terminal can share a desk. The enormous machine can remain outside the window while someone finishes a page. The future has happened, and life has continued within it.
