Thinking in more than one medium

Meaning, models and creative practice

Draw a person beside a doorway. Then draw the doorway so large that the person nearly disappears. The objects are the same; the encounter is not. Put a light inside the opening and the person's relation to it changes again. I can describe those changes in words, but making the image gives them a form my explanation might never have asked for.

That is why creative work matters to my thinking beyond its eventual artifact. An image, a piece of sound or a sequence is not merely a delivery format for an idea already finished elsewhere. The form can make something available to attention that I had not yet understood. I want room to discover that difference before deciding whether it belongs in a story, a design, an argument or a more precise investigation.

Writing has mattered to me in a more personal way, too. Taking it seriously helped me with anxiety. An upsetting experience can consume attention, but it can also become a prompt for creative work. My instinct has been to observe and move on, spending that attention where something can grow, personally or professionally. Writing can be an outlet for that movement.

The diagram asks for a relation

A diagram is often useful because it refuses some of the richness of the situation. Give it events and arrows, and I have to decide what an arrow means. Dependence? Order? A message? A person's inference? When I cannot answer, the drawing has exposed something that an expansive paragraph could keep pleasantly vague. The small representation has made a particular question unavoidable.

Yet the diagram can also become too persuasive. A line drawn between two things makes the connection look settled. Equal circles make different objects appear interchangeable. Putting one box above another suggests a hierarchy that might belong only to the page. I try to keep the act of drawing available to revision: erase an arrow, change a boundary, ask which relation disappeared. In the inquiry into changing descriptions, that difficulty becomes explicit. A language can answer everything it knows how to ask and still leave the needed relation outside it.

Creative work lets me encounter a related difficulty from another direction. What if the relation is visible in an image but has not yet become a clear statement? An illustrated threshold might give me hesitation, invitation or dread before I can say exactly which arrangement produced it. I can develop the composition while trying to understand the effect. Naming it too early may narrow the work to an interpretation the image had already exceeded.

Sound gives a thought an interval

A static mark can be encountered almost at once. Sound asks for an interval. A returning tone, a sustained note or a silence has a relation to what came before and to what I expect next. Consider an imagined instrument that changes its sound when it enters a held state. If every movement produces another alert, I may stop hearing the state and start hearing the interruptions. If the change is too subtle, the relation may never become available at all. The expressive problem is also a problem of attention.

My earlier creator studies explored that relation through programmable geometry, music visualisers, lighting and an expressive lamp. These were ways of asking how one form could become active across different media. A shape could move with sound; an object could reveal its orientation; a light could hold a quiet state. The attraction was not simply to synchronise effects. It was to discover which transformations kept a recognisable character while making the present encounter meaningful.

Music remains part of my personal creative practice, alongside drawing and technical work. I do not need to make each practice justify itself as a product. Nor do I want to flatten them into one universal method: a sound's temporal relation and a picture's spatial relation give me different things to work with. Their exchange becomes useful when I can hear or see the difference, rather than declare in advance that everything is geometry.

A sequence gives the reader some of the work

Now put the person outside the doorway in one frame and inside in the next. Something happened in the interval nobody drew. The reader supplies it. Change the second frame, delay it or place it after a turn of the page, and the supplied relation changes. I find that participation more interesting than a sequence of increasingly spectacular images. The page can make the reader discover something instead of announcing how it should feel.

This is one of the reasons the space between panels matters to the work I am developing in Sleepwalker. The image has a place within an order of attention. A beautiful frame can become the wrong frame if it reveals what the sequence needs to withhold. A quiet detail can become decisive when the reader returns to it with another understanding. The work is partly in the relation the page leaves open.

A narrative has more than one arrangement to hold. An event happened; somebody understood it incorrectly; a reader encountered that account before encountering the event. Revising the reader's discovery need not revise what happened. I am drawn to these several orders of a story because they let an idea retain its consequences while changing how it is encountered. The model gives the author more room to compose. It does not choose what the composition should mean.

Let the crossing change something

A mathematical question can make me notice a visual relation. A traditional passage can give an imagined scene a difficulty to hold. An image can make me ask why a person still matters in an enormous frame. Those crossings are part of one person's inquiry without making all the forms interchangeable. A scene can be exact about what a witness encounters while leaving its world physically impossible. A formal construction can be exact about a small question while leaving most of the witness's life outside it.

I used to reach for large pictures because they gave an unfamiliar thought somewhere to begin. I still want that reach. What changes as the work develops is the willingness to let a form answer back: a calculation exposes the false step, a scene asks for the omitted person, a sequence reveals that the order made the meaning. Each can send me back to the question with something I did not bring into the first attempt.

Keeping those practices alive together is different from deciding that every drawing is research or every curiosity needs a public project. Some work should remain play. Some questions need the patience of study. Some acquire a useful technical form; others become more honest through an image. I want a working life in which I can follow those changes without asking one title to explain them all.

The artifact is where somebody else may eventually meet the inquiry. Before that, making it is already a way of thinking. The doorway can remain a doorway while becoming something I did not know I was trying to understand.
