What I want a memory to do

Meaning, models and creative practice

Suppose I write something down because I cannot yet understand why it interests me. Months later, another question arrives and the earlier thought becomes useful. The note did not improve while it waited. Something changed in the relation between what I had kept and what I was trying to understand. That is the part of memory I find most difficult to design for: I would like to preserve the possibility without preserving everything as though everything were equally important.

It is easy to confuse the size of an archive with the reach of a mind. More material can give a question more to meet. It can also give an old explanation more ways to keep returning. A memory that retrieves the same persuasive account whenever I encounter difficulty may help me continue, or prevent me from noticing that the difficulty has changed. What I want from it is closer to a productive encounter than to a reliable repetition.

A note waiting for its question

Imagine keeping a description of how two pieces of equipment connect. For months, the description is less useful than an ordinary stock count. Then a repair fails because the available replacement does not fit. The old connection detail becomes decisive. The memory did not need to predict the exact repair in order to retain something valuable, but the retained detail still needs a route into the new question. An archive can contain it and leave the person asking about it none the wiser.

My interest in this goes beyond searching by a better phrase. A new question may reorganise what the old material means. The word “available” might once have meant “present in the store”; it now has to mean “usable for this machine”. The stock-and-compatibility construction makes that change visible. If my account has no place for compatibility, a more confident answer about stock does not help. I need to revisit the description, not just retrieve a better value within it.

This is also what makes rereading worthwhile. I can return to the same passage with a question I did not have before and notice a relation that familiarity concealed. The words remain, yet the encounter changes. A note is most valuable to me when it can reconnect me with that possibility: enough of the source, enough of the question I was following, enough of what I had not settled. A conclusion detached from all three can be memorably phrased and remarkably hard to use.

Three different ways to change

When an experience does not fit my expectation, several responses are possible. I can keep the exception alongside the account. I can adjust the account. Or I can discover that its categories were too small for the question. Those responses operate at different depths. Treating every exception as a reason to rebuild everything makes learning unstable; treating every exception as another item to file makes the original explanation almost impossible to disturb.

The distinction has become a research question for me. In MUON's surprise inquiry, retaining something, changing a learned parameter and proposing a structural change are separate responses. I am interested in the choice between them. A discrepancy is valuable because it creates work for the explanation, not because its intensity tells us which explanation should replace it. The inconvenient observation may be a mistake, a rare event, a changed situation or the missing distinction that makes the rest intelligible.

That keeps the ambition alive without making the machinery a portrait of a person. A learning experiment can define precisely what a retained cue, an update or a change of structure means in its small world. My own experience is messier: I do not have a loss function for everything I care about, and usefulness can change because my commitments changed. The experiment gives one part of the difficulty a form I can examine. It does not need to explain the whole of learning to be worth doing.

Useful after the moment has passed

A cue can matter long after the response that preserved it. Suppose a system sees a signal early, receives an unrelated stream, and is asked much later to act on that signal. A method that excels at the intervening work can still fail because it discarded the one earlier distinction the later task needed. I find this a more revealing memory problem than asking whether a stored phrase can be repeated immediately. The interval gives retention a consequence.

Reckon gives that interest a laboratory setting: retained records and their use can be varied within its benchmark tasks. The related question of updating after feedback asks how a later outcome should change an earlier estimate. An old success need not outweigh a changed situation indefinitely; a recent failure need not erase everything before it. The shape of the update expresses what we think the history still tells us.

Outside the laboratory, I often want to keep an unfinished thought because it may meet another field later. That possibility is a reason to retain its distinctive question, not to call every collected fragment important. A future connection needs something specific to connect with. When I compress a thought to “interesting idea about learning”, I have preserved my enthusiasm and discarded most of what another inquiry could use.

What difference did remembering make?

Finding the note feels like success because something familiar returned. The more interesting question is what I could do after finding it. Did it expose the assumption I had forgotten? Did it change a decision? Did it save me from repeating a failed approach, or simply lend another quotation to the approach I already favoured? An opened document is a weak answer to those questions.

Scope brings that question into the working environment I am building. I want it to remember more than relevant passages: the people around a task, the long game of a project and the context that makes the same words mean something different in another relationship. The descriptive picture should help an agent meet the situation without making it a permanent judgment about the people in it.

This is the pressure behind learning which context actually helps. Frequency can tell a system what I tend to retrieve; it cannot by itself tell the system whether my habit was useful. Something rarely used can be indispensable when its particular condition appears. Something frequently opened can have become a comfortable distraction. I want a memory that becomes sensitive to that difference, rather than one that makes my previous preferences harder to escape.

The technical ambition brings me back to an ordinary personal difficulty. A thought can survive as a sentence and lose the question that made it alive. It can return so familiarly that I mistake recognition for help. I do not want every unfinished thought to become a lifelong obligation. I want the ones that survive to carry enough of their encounter that, when another question arrives, they offer more than the fact that I once found them interesting.
