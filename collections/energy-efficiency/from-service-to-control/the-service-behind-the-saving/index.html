The service behind the saving

Switch the equipment off and the consumption chart looks wonderful. We have found a very effective way to save electricity, and a rather poor way to cool a building. The absurd case is easy to see. It becomes less obvious when the pump is still running, the saving is smaller and the part of the building losing service is far from the screen.

Working on energy products made me suspicious of a lower number offered without its surrounding work. What did the plant deliver before, and what does it deliver now? Once that question enters the comparison, a saving becomes a much more interesting engineering problem.

What did we keep cooling?

In cooling, the output is not simply that a machine is running. The spaces being served need an acceptable result under particular conditions, which means two readings cannot be compared fairly just because they have the same unit. If the load, weather, operating hours or service requirement differs, the comparison may contain more than the effect of the intervention. A department closing, a load being outsourced or equipment operating for fewer hours can all lower consumption without improving efficiency. These can be sensible decisions, but they answer a different question from doing the same work with less energy. A product that wants to advise people has to distinguish these mechanisms, or it learns to praise whatever makes the number smaller.

The pump borrows work from the rest of the plant

A cooling system makes the boundary problem concrete. Changing a pump changes flow, which can alter the work at the chiller and the service delivered elsewhere. A cooling tower can consume more while allowing another component to consume less. The intervention must therefore be understood across the coupled operation: looking only at the device adjusted can miss both a benefit elsewhere and a harm elsewhere. The measurement boundary follows the question we are asking, rather than the convenience of the available meter.

The physical dependencies also complicate aggregation. A main meter and its subloads are not independent quantities to add. Two interventions can affect the same consumption, even if they have different names and appear on different pages of a report. Deciding what can be counted together is part of determining which improvement the engineering is trying to produce, not an accounting detail left until afterwards.

The experiment that changes the next experiment

An attractive intervention deserves a trial because the coupled behaviour is part of what we need to learn. Closing a valve and changing a pump setting are not equivalent interventions, even if both appear to concern the same flow. A suspected bottleneck may not be the limiting one; a component may behave differently from its nominal curve; the operating team may identify a service constraint the analysis missed. Testing the first arrangement can reveal why a second experiment should include another part of the system.

The possibility of stopping gives the trial value. A change that looks promising at one load may be inappropriate at another. Discovering that condition can improve the next experiment or show that continuing the disruption is unjustified. This is one reason I value the connection between product work and field observation: the field helps determine what the design should become. Retaining what was tried, what was observed and what condition changed gives the next experiment something to work from, without turning every discarded intervention into either an embarrassment or a delivered result.

A few hours on the chart, a year in the spreadsheet

A short observation can support a comparison for the hours it covers. It does not become a year of performance because a spreadsheet multiplies it by operating hours. Someone deciding whether to invest still needs an estimate of future benefit, but that estimate depends on the assumed hours, load, tariff, maintenance and continuation of the service. Keeping the measured comparison recognisable within the forecast prevents its uncertainty from disappearing exactly when the claim becomes larger.

Before-and-after charts need the same attention to their conditions. If the meter, the baseline or the way readings were collected changed, that belongs in the interpretation; a persuasive line cannot isolate a cause that the comparison did not isolate. Making those conditions available is a product challenge too. An operator should be able to understand why a comparison is relevant without having to reconstruct the analysis behind it.

Who pays for the saving?

Energy services add another layer. A provider may invest in equipment and continuing operation in exchange for a share of savings. The customer may avoid capital expense while accepting a particular service relationship. Physical energy reduction, avoided maintenance expense and tariff-dependent monetary saving are not identical benefits: a lower bill can reflect a price change, and an efficient plant can still encounter a higher tariff. The arrangement therefore needs an agreed baseline and a way to handle changed loads and periods. Those agreements connect the physical benefit to its commercial value; calculating a saving does not by itself produce an accepted report, an invoice or a payment.

My work on product and technology supported that continuing operation, alongside the teams responsible for energy services and commercial delivery. Installation began a relationship with the plant: observations, changed conditions and the customer's experience continued to affect what counted as a useful result.

When the chart starts giving instructions

Once a metric is visible and rewarded, people have a reason to optimise it. That makes its definition part of the product's behaviour. A system that celebrates consumption alone can encourage service loss; one that celebrates the number of successful commands can discourage investigation into what those commands achieved. The measure affects the work, so keeping it connected to the service is part of the design rather than a qualification added to the report.

Energy work gave me a concrete way to see this relationship between a measurable input and a contextual output. I find it useful in software too, though the mechanisms differ: a faster response can leave more work unfinished, or a lower ticket count can result from customers giving up. Understanding the work behind the number is what makes the comparison useful. In the plant, that means knowing what service continued before deciding what the saving was worth.
