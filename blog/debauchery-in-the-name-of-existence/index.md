# Debauchery, in the name of existence\!

*A provocation from the end of 2019.*

History. Sound. Ideas. What do they have in common? I kept seeing cycles: peace and violence, crests and troughs, a return that our confident accounts of progress had not prepared us to recognise. Periodicity seemed almost a property of existence itself. Life was a montage of the ripples that existence left behind, and we were rarely still enough to notice what repeated.

It remained important to strive for peace. Oblivious to the ripples, the cycle continued.

<a id="are-we-solving-the-problem"></a>

## Are we solving the problem?

We take pride in calling ourselves problem solvers, innovators and explorers. But if a wave washes the sand from beneath my feet while I watch the sunset, does witnessing the spectacle make me an explorer?

Why ask such an absurd question? Meh. No reason. Except that I wanted to challenge the flattering premise beneath those names.

What if much of what we called solving a problem was a transformation of its presentation? We took an unpleasant present and moved it into a less familiar, more enticing future. The future inherited the consequences, while the present celebrated the ingenuity of the translation.

That was not necessarily easy work. It could require extraordinary engineering, organisation and imagination. The objection was that being difficult did not settle whether it had solved the problem. A civilisation could become very accomplished at changing the form in which its difficulties arrived.

Businesspeople, engineers, doctors, lawyers, teachers, scientists and statespeople all participated. We divided the planet with fences, colours, weapons and beliefs, then kept fighting the wars of people no longer alive. Generations of martyrs raised further generations. When trust became scarce, cooperation looked increasingly like another game. Somehow, like cockroaches, we survived.

<a id="the-uncomfortable-possibility-of-being-wrong"></a>

## The uncomfortable possibility of being wrong

I thought of Phoebe challenging Ross in *Friends*: could he leave even a little room for the possibility that he was wrong? The appeal was in the challenge to his certainty. A sitcom exchange is a poor history of science, but it can be an excellent mirror for intellectual vanity.

Artists seemed more willing than many of us to admit that they did not know what the hell they were doing. An artist could stay inside a question without first dressing it as a result. I found that courage valuable.

Enlarge the window beyond one lifetime and our professional confidence can begin to look peculiar. We understand some intricate details and still struggle with the elementary questions. What is consciousness? Do the needs of the many outweigh those of the few? What is the nature of existence? We move between quantities, convictions and competing ways of giving the question meaning.

I was frustrated by how quickly that difficulty could become a reason to stop listening. Citations could be treated as a substitute for understanding the claim they supported. A reference to a Vedic practice could be dismissed before anyone had asked what kind of account it offered.

<a id="which-vocabulary-gets-to-enter-the-conversation"></a>

## Which vocabulary gets to enter the conversation?

Consider the five koshas, or sheaths, described in Vedantic accounts of a person. Or the yogic vocabulary of ida, pingala and sushumna. These names do not become anatomical structures simply because someone draws a parallel with nerves or glands. Yet I wanted room to ask what the descriptions were doing before deciding that their failure to fit an anatomical vocabulary made them worthless.

My provocation was that science, too, was another belief system, albeit one in which consistency and elegance were paramount. I wanted to challenge the confidence with which we could dismiss an unfamiliar account while treating our own frame as the only one worth inhabiting. The comparison did not establish that every inherited claim was true. If we could be sceptical of an unfamiliar claim, surely we could also examine the certainty with which we had decided what was worth hearing.

The [question of vocabulary](https://getharsh.in/blog/an-analogy-can-start-an-argument/) irritated me: how could this conversation recognise its own assumptions if its words already settled what could count as an answer?

<a id="the-thing-we-let-loose"></a>

## The thing we let loose

We create technologies and ideologies, then release them into a world whose responses we cannot fully anticipate. Greed can outrun compassion. Confidence in the evidence we possess can outrun our understanding of what will happen once other people begin using the creation.

Should we create something knowing that it could be used for great evil? The question deserves more than a template answer. A calculation can illuminate a consequence, but somebody still chooses what belongs in the calculation. A belief can sustain a commitment, but somebody still lives with what that commitment permits.

We no longer have to stone a philosopher to make a difficult question unwelcome. There are quieter ways to keep it out of the room. Oblivious to the ripples, the cycle continues.

<a id="the-silence-between-the-vibrations"></a>

## The silence between the vibrations

We seek symmetry in history, sound and ideas. I was interested in the stretches of silence between the vibrations: what the impression of continuity made us overlook. A coherent story could offer a sense of knowledge and control while hiding how much we had simply carried forward into another form.

That was my worry about innovation. Beyond the question of securing the next investment, what had we understood about the problem's survival? Had we changed the conditions producing it, or transferred the burden to a person or generation outside the frame?

A game organised around survival does not automatically give us exuberance. Yet we keep rolling the dice, enjoying the next little rush, while the possibility of losing remains somebody else's difficulty. Debauchery, in the name of existence.

All that anger came back to a modest wish: more compassion, and a more peaceful new year. Solving a problem ought to include the lives that continue after our account of the solution ends. I wanted us to look at those lives before celebrating how cleverly we had moved the problem.
