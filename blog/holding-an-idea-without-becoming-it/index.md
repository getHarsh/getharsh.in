# Holding an idea without becoming it

When I wrote about [a school of unlearning](https://getharsh.in/collections/learning-and-practice/unlearning-needs-something-to-test-against/), detachment from the outcome was central to the idea. I wanted to follow that thought into a less expected setting: capitalism. My argument is that detachment can make business more cooperative, because it lets us notice possibilities that a contest over our identities keeps closing.

Bring an idea into a room and watch what happens. Someone says it will never work, and suddenly you are defending much more than the proposal. There were months behind it, perhaps a career decision, perhaps the hope of finally building something of your own. They seem to have dismissed all of that in one sentence. Or did they? They may have noticed a dependency you missed. The conversation has become personal before either of you has worked out what the objection was.

That ordinary, expensive confusion is part of why detachment interests me. I want to care deeply about the work without making it responsible for proving who I am. It sounds straightforward until the idea on the table is mine.

<a id="the-drive-that-becomes-attachment"></a>

## The drive that becomes attachment

In the original 2024 essay, I called trauma the root of human motivation and creativity, and argued that it could drive extraordinary work when channelled well. That was the psychological hypothesis behind my business argument. I was trying to understand what happens when an experience shakes the beliefs a person has built their life around: a betrayal, a humiliation, a loss, or a rejection that looks trivial to everybody else.

The size of the event from outside does not tell us how deeply it reaches into that person’s beliefs. Calling someone a snowflake avoids that question. Hurt can become a reason to make something happen, to prove oneself or to build a different life. Attachment to that purpose can supply enormous persistence. It can also make an unprofitable or unsustainable path feel impossible to leave, because changing the plan now feels like surrendering the person it helped us become.

That is the tension I wanted detachment to address. The drive can remain while the plan changes. I can want to build something without asking its success to settle every earlier hurt. Otherwise a business disagreement inherits a much older argument, and the person across the table may have no idea they have joined it.

<a id="what-did-they-actually-say"></a>

## What did they actually say?

Imagine showing someone a plan for a new product. They say, “This will never work.” Perhaps they see a genuine distribution problem. Perhaps they have seen something superficially similar fail and are importing that history. Perhaps they are frustrated with the way the proposal was presented. The response can concern the idea, reveal the speaker's assumptions and comment on the person receiving it, all within the same sentence. Separating those possibilities gives the discussion an object we can examine.

There are two tempting shortcuts: dismiss the speaker because they do not understand, or treat the force of their response as evidence that the proposal is wrong. Both avoid separating the objection from the story surrounding it. Asking “What specifically would prevent it from working?” gives us something to examine. An answer about customer behaviour can be investigated; an answer about a missing capability can change the plan. Even a vague objection can reveal that the idea has not been communicated well enough. Detachment creates room for that examination without supplying its answer.

<a id="calibrate-for-the-error"></a>

## Calibrate for the error

Robotics gives me a useful comparison. The command I send and the motion an actuator produces can differ. If I insist that my command is ground truth about the machine, the machine will never work as I intend. I need to observe its response and understand the error. The point of calibration is to let the difference change what I do next.

A conversation also has a transmission, a medium and a reception. I know what I meant to say; the other person received something through particular words, timing and circumstances. Their response is evidence about that encounter. It is not a direct readout of my intention, any more than my command is a measurement of the actuator's motion. People bring histories and meanings that the machine analogy cannot contain, but the comparison gives me a useful place to start: what crossed the gap, and what did I assume had crossed it?

I cannot diagnose the person from their objection. I need enough distance to distinguish criticism of the idea, the speaker's assumptions and a comment on me. They are three different things, even when I initially hear them as one attack.

That is the calibration I want to practise. Asking which part of the proposal failed to convince someone gives the conversation something more useful than defending the whole of myself. Not everything is about me. An uncomfortable truth, and occasionally a considerable relief.

<a id="listening-without-preparing-the-defence"></a>

## Listening without preparing the defence

There is a peculiar form of listening in which I hear only enough to construct my reply. I am present in the conversation, but most of my attention is already in the argument I intend to make. The cost is informational as well as interpersonal: the objection might contain the part that would have saved the team weeks of effort, and I am too busy defeating it to notice.

A similar investment can gather around an explanation. If a picture of time made a difficult question feel intelligible, finding a flaw in it can seem to threaten the question itself. I might protect the picture by explaining away the flaw, or dismiss the whole inquiry because its first formulation failed. Both responses give that formulation too much power. I can let [the explanation change](https://getharsh.in/blog/an-analogy-can-start-an-argument/) while continuing to ask what made the question worth following. The distinction gives curiosity somewhere to go after the attractive answer stops helping.

I can disagree with an objection while still letting it finish. I can revise a mechanism without surrendering the purpose. I can retain an intuition while admitting that the current explanation is inadequate. Not everything has to be a zero-sum game, or a choice between being entirely right and entirely wrong. As Obi-Wan would have it, only the Sith deal in absolutes. Humans are more than zero and one.

<a id="two-firms-one-useful-possibility"></a>

## Two firms, one useful possibility

Detachment also changes how I think about cooperation in business. A transaction can divide something that already exists, but a collaboration can make something possible that neither side could produce alone. Suppose one small firm understands the customer's operation and another can implement a missing component. Combining those strengths could produce useful work, yet each may worry that contributing knowledge will make the other stronger at its expense.

That concern can be real: a contract, a commercial boundary or the decision not to collaborate may be necessary. There is also a different obstacle, when each firm becomes so committed to being the complete solution that neither recognises the other's contribution. They protect their standing and lose the opportunity. Distance helps me distinguish what I need from the identity I have built around how I obtain it. Owning every component, winning every argument and receiving every credit are not always the same as achieving the outcome I care about.

This is the sense in which I say detachment complements capitalism. It makes room for a non-zero-sum game: the useful work we can create together may be larger than the advantage either of us can protect alone. I do not need everyone to become a hermit for that to work. I need the question “What can we make possible?” to remain audible when “What is mine?” starts shouting over it.

Recognising that possibility does not mean trusting every promise. A collaboration can displace costs, reflect unequal bargaining power or conceal exploitation behind apparently generous cooperation. Whether it creates additional value, who receives it and what each participant knowingly accepts need examination. Those questions ask more of us than either “competition is bad” or “the market will sort it out.”

<a id="keeping-the-purpose-changing-the-plan"></a>

## Keeping the purpose, changing the plan

Detachment is sometimes presented as withdrawal from ambition. I find that too simple. A leader who can change a plan may be more committed to the mission than one who cannot. A founder who lets a vehicle end may [preserve more of the original purpose](https://getharsh.in/collections/building-products-and-teams/what-remains-when-an-experiment-ends/) than one who keeps it going on promises it can no longer keep. The form can change while the commitment survives.

Changing direction at every objection is not openness either. An idea needs enough persistence to encounter reality; some criticism is mistaken, and some resistance reflects a worthwhile disruption. Distance should make those reasons clearer rather than make commitment impossible. I have to listen, separate the claims, return to the purpose and decide, then stand behind the decision without turning it into a permanent portrait of who I am.

<a id="the-person-across-the-table"></a>

## The person across the table

People speak from different histories. A past failure can explain the urgency of an objection without settling whether it applies to this proposal. I can take that urgency seriously and still ask what, specifically, it reveals about the work. The person does not have to be reduced to a psychological explanation for the idea to receive a fair hearing.

Cooperation needs participants who can distinguish the shared work from a contest over themselves, as well as compatible capabilities. Making room for the other person's account, asking for the concrete concern and returning together to the purpose are everyday ways of practising that distinction. Detachment does not remove the stakes; it gives us a chance to see what they actually are.
