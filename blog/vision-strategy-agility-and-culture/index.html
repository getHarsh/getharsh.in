Vision, strategy, agility and culture

On this page

  5. Use the information to reach an agreement

“Agile is broken.” “Culture eats strategy for breakfast.” “Vision matters.” We hear the phrases so often that they begin to resemble explanations. But what do they help us change on Monday morning?

I do not think agility is broken. I think it is frequently misunderstood. Vision, strategy, agility and culture have different work to do, and the useful question is how they meet inside a team trying to deliver something.

A vision needs the surrounding detail

A bold vision should make me a little uncomfortable. It asks the team and me to go beyond what we already know how to do. That reach can be valuable, provided I also take responsibility for the details around it: what, why, how and when.

Strategy is the work of making choices around the ambition. Inspirational quotations can remind us to choose, make trade-offs, work hard, revise after failure or look at the result. They cannot do those things for the particular problem in front of us.

The real world will introduce variables the plan did not account for. The larger the undertaking and the longer the timeline, the more room there is for that to happen. A roadmap is drawn while the target and our understanding of it are still moving.

Leadership has to give people something stable enough to work towards without pretending that the surroundings have become static. The tools and the explanation matter. Repeating the vision more emphatically is not a method for absorbing a changed assumption.

Defining the problem takes time too

I have found planning along a logarithmic scale useful: greater uncertainty calls for coarser estimates, with more detail becoming available as the work progresses. The attraction is the recognition that the first estimate and the later one are made with different knowledge.

Developers can estimate the time required to design a solution while overlooking the time required to define the problem. Those are connected pieces of work. A missing constraint can change the solution; an attempted solution can reveal that the problem was described incorrectly.

My rule of thumb is simple: do not make a promise at the first encounter with a problem. Ask enough to understand what the promise would include. As details emerge, the account becomes more granular. Defining and solving the problem proceed back and forth.

That is where agility matters. It gives us a way to learn without treating every discovery as somebody's failure to obey the original plan. The cone of uncertainty is one picture of this changing relationship between knowledge and estimation. It is useful as a reminder to examine uncertainty, not as a guarantee that uncertainty will shrink on schedule.

A plan with room to move

Design and mathematics can help a plan absorb change while preserving the team's ability to execute. A dependency can be made visible. A decision can be postponed until the information needed for it arrives. Work can be divided so that revising one part does not require everyone to abandon what they were doing.

I think of this as elasticity. The target moves, the plan absorbs some of the movement, and the team does not lose all its momentum. A pivot that repeatedly discards the team's context consumes more than the time shown in the next schedule.

A cross-functional, self-organising team needs enough shared understanding to participate in that adjustment. People should be able to recognise why something changed and what still holds. Otherwise, self-organisation becomes a polite name for asking them to guess which instruction matters today.

Culture carries what the process cannot repeat

Processes help make work consistent. They can also become mechanical. A team cannot stop to consult a policy for every small act of cooperation, every admission of uncertainty or every moment when somebody needs help.

Culture carries some of that work. It gives people expectations about what happens when they raise a concern, revise an estimate or discover that another part of the team already solved their problem. A framework can name the behaviour; the everyday response teaches whether it is safe and useful to do it.

When the stakes are high, people-operations expertise can help a team design its own working arrangements. The point is not to impose a second strategy in the name of culture. It is to help the team develop the practices through which the strategy can become ordinary work.

In that sense, strategy is homework I do to help the right culture become possible. The design of the work and the life of the team keep affecting one another.

Use the information to reach an agreement

Nine women cannot deliver a baby in one month. The old project-management analogy survives because the mistake survives: adding people does not divide every duration. Some work depends on a sequence, some on accumulated understanding and some on a process that cannot be accelerated by changing the headcount.

Collecting data is not enough. I want to use it to help the team agree on what is happening and what to do next. An observation can make a discussion more concrete than an argument over what someone intended by a phrase in a meeting.

But data does not interpret itself. We choose what to collect and which comparison to make. Confirmation bias can follow us into the dashboard. The useful discipline is to let the evidence change the shared account, including the leader's account, rather than use the numbers as another way to win the room.

The thing the framework needs

Vision supplies direction. Strategy makes choices around it. Agility lets the work learn. Culture shapes how people live with those choices and discoveries.

None of this works well without trust. Leadership includes building a shared vision that the team can actually carry, then supporting the people who carry it. They need room to contribute their knowledge, including knowledge that makes the original plan less comfortable.

As with most good things, patience is part of the job. A framework can help us practise it. It cannot practise it on our behalf.
