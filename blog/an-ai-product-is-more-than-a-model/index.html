From a model answer to a useful product

On this page

My earlier encounters with AI came through image processing and embedded systems. The model did its part inside an arrangement of sensors, software and people. It didn't usually offer to explain the arrangement back to me.

A language model does. Ask it a question and the component suddenly sounds like the person who understands the whole operation. It can interpret a request, explain a bill and recommend a next step. That is an extraordinary entrance to a product. But what happens when we follow the recommendation past the answer? The bill has to be the right one. The proposed correction has to reach a process that can make it. Somebody still has to live with the result.

The customer didn't arrive for our demonstration

In a demonstration, we can provide the right information, ask an intelligible question and stop at the impressive answer. A customer arrives with a situation. They may have the wrong record, misunderstand the procedure or ask for something the business cannot do. They may be returning after a failed attempt, or need somebody else's approval before acting. Their starting point is part of the product problem.

Consider a hypothetical small business trying to understand an unexpected charge. A model can explain a bill, but I would first want to decide what kind of help the product is offering. Is it an explanation, a prepared dispute or a change to the account? These are different promises. The prepared dispute might be a useful first product: identify the bill, show the disputed amount and its basis, let the customer correct the draft, then put it into an existing resolution process.

That choice changes what we build and measure. A fluent explanation of the wrong bill cannot pass merely because it reads well. A correct draft that never reaches anyone able to resolve it has also left the customer with unfinished work. Before expanding the conversation to every question about the account, I would want the narrow operation to have a recognisable end: the customer can inspect the proposed dispute and knows what happened after submitting it.

What becomes possible when the entrance changes?

The new capability can change the process around it. A business that previously required customers to know its internal categories might let them begin with an outcome. A worker might examine a larger range of alternatives because preparing each one costs less. This changes how people ask for help, what work is economical to offer and where human attention is needed, rather than merely making the old screen faster.

The move from broadcast to on-demand gives a useful comparison. Distribution technology changed what people could consume and how creators could reach them. A conversational system could likewise change the entrance to a service: the person states an outcome instead of first learning the provider's categories. The product opportunity includes that new relationship. But possibility is not adoption. A useful behaviour has to meet an actual need, and the organisation needs a way to sustain it. A feature that customers try once because it is surprising is different from work they return to because it helps.

A smaller product around a larger capability

At Zenoti, customer evidence led me towards a simpler experience for a small-business segment within a much larger suite. Understanding what the customer needed mattered more than displaying everything the product could do. AI introduces different mechanisms, but a similar product decision: a model may make many responses possible while the valuable operation remains narrow. The user might need to settle one charge, not converse about the whole account. Three consequential choices can be more useful than a seemingly unlimited assistant that cannot complete any of them.

Support, onboarding, data migration and handoffs are part of that operation. They determine whether the person can begin, whether the context is coherent and whether an exception has somewhere to go. A conversational front desk still needs those connections behind it; fluent conversation does not remove the work of completing a request.

Which part needs to get better?

A poor result can come from several places: the model misunderstands, retrieval selects the wrong source, a tool returns stale information or the business process is undefined. Treating every failure as a model problem lets us spend more on the component while leaving the cause untouched. Better training can address a recurring capability gap; a clearer source can address a retrieval problem; an explicit rule can resolve a decision the business has already settled. A narrower task or a person's judgment may be the right intervention for an exception. Technical fluency helps product work choose among these mechanisms instead of translating every objection into a demand for a more intelligent model.

Research, adaptation and operation have different work to do. Research may explore whether a capability is possible. Adaptation makes a chosen capability fit a task and its examples. Operation has to keep the resulting application usable as sources, users and dependencies change. A roadmap connects these activities through the outcome it seeks, while giving each an appropriate way to learn. Treating them as one uninterrupted process of “the AI getting better” makes both costs and responsibilities harder to see.

The economic comparison also has to follow the completed work. A cheaper call can create more retries and support, while a more expensive model can be worthwhile if it reliably removes a costly part of the process. Neither conclusion follows from the price per token alone. Specialised capability, different models within one application and deterministic software each have a place where they improve the work. The model does not need to become everything for the product to become useful.

What am I confirming when I press the button?

When an application suggests a correction to the hypothetical bill, the person needs to know what is being changed and why. A confirmation button without that context only makes the ambiguity clickable. Improving the system presents a related problem: a rating can report dissatisfaction without locating its cause, and a trace can record steps without explaining which statement is justified. In both cases, the interface needs to make the consequential parts available to judgment. That may call for a comparison, an editable draft, a source reference or a handoff, rather than another conversational turn. Reducing effort helps when it leaves the person better able to complete the task; concealing a consequential mistake takes something else away.

Beyond the impressive answer

An AI product brings a capability into an outcome someone wants, at a cost the operation can carry, with a way to recognise and repair the failures that matter. Agents and conversation contribute through the useful work they make possible. Product work, architecture and engineering meet in making that promise specific enough for somebody to try and the result specific enough to learn from. What interests me is the work that becomes possible, the people it helps and the arrangement around the model that makes it real.

The broader argument about how this changes product management follows the research, the new user behaviours and the organisation needed to build for them.
