The vocabulary of an unfamiliar world

On this page

  2. When the picture becomes the explanation
  3. A lifetime in a breath

Okay, imagine joining a conversation where nobody shares your vocabulary. You can point, draw and demonstrate, but even those gestures need some agreement. A circle might mean a place, a person, a boundary or a journey. Where do you begin? Before arguing about the answer, you have to discover what the other person thinks the question is.

I keep running into a version of that predicament when I think about time. We have clocks, equations and a perfectly serviceable language for getting to a meeting. Then somebody asks what time is, and the meeting suddenly seems easier. What have those words and symbols allowed us to describe? What did we leave outside them?

A triangle with three right angles

Draw a triangle on a flat sheet. Its angles add to 180 degrees. Now start at the North Pole, follow a meridian to the equator, travel a quarter of the way around the equator and return along another meridian. The three angles of this spherical triangle are all right angles. The result is 270 degrees. Did the triangle break mathematics?

The right angles are measured on the spherical surface. Its projection onto this page changes how those angles look.

Of course not. We changed the setting and kept speaking as though we hadn't. The familiar statement came with a surface, a meaning of a straight path and a set of assumptions. On the sphere, the sides follow great-circle arcs. The surprise is useful because it makes the setting visible again. What looked like a fact about every possible triangle was a fact about a particular geometry.

That does something to my intuition about time. Perhaps a familiar picture has become so comfortable that I no longer notice what it assumes. A line orders events. A clock compares intervals. A dependency diagram shows which event relied on which other event. Waiting for a reply gives me yet another encounter with duration. These are useful descriptions, but they do not all make the same thing visible.

It is tempting to jump from the spherical triangle to a different geometry for time. I have felt that temptation. But the triangle has not told us what such a geometry would be, much less what it would predict. It has given us a better question: am I dissatisfied with the physical account, the picture I use to understand it, or the way it meets experience? Those lead to rather different work.

My early essay More than a line pushed the image into a deliberately strange thought experiment: suppose time had a fractional dimension and could be described in a non-Euclidean geometry. What would a line through that space look like? I imagined it bending, looping, disappearing and returning before it began. The image led me towards retrocausality, the possibility of an effect preceding its cause, and towards a hunch that time, chaos, entropy and consciousness belonged in a connected inquiry.

I had not constructed that geometry. Curvature, fractional dimension and causal ordering name different properties; choosing either of the first two does not establish the third. That is where the mathematical work has to begin: define the space, the dimension being used, the relation between events and what “before” means within the construction. A second, a minute and an hour give me units for comparing duration. They do not, by themselves, settle which of those questions I meant by “what does time look like?” I still want to follow the hunch far enough that its assumptions can meet a real test.

When the picture becomes the explanation

Mathematics gives us an extraordinary vocabulary. A derivative makes a rate of change something we can work with; a coordinate system lets us describe a position; a transformation lets us ask what survives a change of description. Learning the notation is not quite the same as learning to see through it. A beautifully written equation can remain as opaque as that conversation in an unfamiliar language.

I am interested in the point where a representation becomes something we can think with. Could a better description of dependence make an otherwise confusing process intelligible? Could it expose that two accounts agree only because we have forgotten how their variables were measured? Those are questions about the work a representation does, before any grand claim about discovering a new law.

My questions have become more particular as I have followed them. The early image asked whether a different vocabulary could make a difficult world intelligible. The research question now asks what a description lets me distinguish, what a change of setting does to those distinctions and how I can recognise that its language has become too small. Changing the description itself is a larger ambition than improving an estimate within it. A perfectly accurate answer can still concern the wrong-sized question.

Take two exchanges that overlap in time. A timestamp tells me when each request and reply occurred. It may leave out which reply answered which request, whether the answer resolved the question, and which later decision relied on it. A different picture can restore those relations without changing the physical intervals. If I then ask what would remain valid after a reply were corrected, the picture has another job: it has to preserve the dependencies through which the correction matters. The useful vocabulary changes as the work demands more of it.

That is the progression I want to keep following. An image suggests a question; a construction makes one part of it precise; a counterexample shows where the construction stops helping. I do not want to turn an awkward question into a polite instruction to stay within familiar categories, or ask the first metaphor to defend every explanation I attach to it. A different formalism would need to let us do something we could not do as well before. The reach of the image can survive the work of finding out what that something is.

A lifetime in a breath

Now change the observer. Imagine a lifetime passing in a breath on another scale. From inside that life, there were years of waiting, work, love and loss. From outside, barely a moment. The image unsettles me because nothing about the smaller world feels small to the person living there.

That is part of what draws me towards Markandeya recognising his own place within an impossible frame. A story can let the reader inhabit the shift. An equation would need to define its quantities and their relationship; the scene can stay with what the change of scale does to a person's sense of a life. It has a different kind of precision: which detail remains recognisable when the world around it becomes impossible?

I want both forms available. Sometimes the next step is to calculate. Sometimes it is to sit with an image until I understand why it will not leave me alone.

An operating system for intelligence?

The same habit follows me into product work. What if intelligence could be composed the way we compose software? The operating-system image opens a whole vocabulary: capabilities, interfaces, memory, applications. Suddenly there are places to ask where a useful piece belongs and how something else could use it.

Some of that has an ordinary software answer. Retain the result of a step, expose an interface and resume when the missing information arrives. That was part of the work of composing agents and playbooks at DAO Studio. Another part of the ambition reaches inside a learned model: can we find a capacity, separate it and use it elsewhere? A callback does not answer that question. We would have to understand what the capacity depends on and what changes when we move it.

Still, I would rather follow the discomfort than discard the image. Perhaps the useful abstraction is an application interface. Perhaps the capacity we hoped to compose depends on an exchange the interface never described. The comparison has helped me find both questions, provided I let the unfamiliar work change the familiar name's meaning.

That is what interests me about vocabulary. It can give a thought somewhere to begin, and then quietly become the wall it cannot get past. The answer is not always another word. Sometimes we need a different picture, an experiment or a story. Sometimes we need to return to the conversation and admit that we have been answering different questions all along.

What a different picture lets us ask

Imagine sending two requests before either reply arrives. The clock records when each request and reply occurred, but neither reply names the request it answers. Putting the four events in order still leaves two possible exchanges. A diagram could show which reply belongs to which request, provided we have a source for that connection. The worked example on Causality shows why more precise timestamps cannot supply the missing relation.

Now imagine claiming that the alternative picture predicts a different interval between request and reply. That claim asks more of the picture. It needs a definition of the predicted quantity and an observation that could distinguish it from the ordinary account. A representation that reveals dependence is useful for that purpose; its usefulness does not automatically become evidence for the new prediction.

Experience raises a third question. Waiting for an important reply can feel longer than doing absorbing work during an equal measured interval. The clock gives us a common comparison; the description of waiting gives us something about the person's situation. Neither is improved by pretending to replace the other. The interesting work is to understand what kind of difference the person is describing.

The dependency picture, a predicted interval and the experience of waiting consequently ask different things of the same exchange. The first needs the missing relation, the second needs a physical model and observations, and the third needs the person's situation. They can meet in one event while requiring different ways of making it intelligible.
