# The future we create, and condemn

*From January 2020. The original title was “The only way to be certain of the future is to create it. A surer way is to condemn it.”*

What do we know about the past beyond the fact that it is not the present? Its ghosts haunt us, but so do the ghosts of the future's past: things that have not happened yet, which we already imagine remembering with regret. Somewhere between them, we can become the very thing we condemn.

If I wanted an image of collective consciousness, I would look at society. We are social animals, although we seem to have wandered very far into the woods around the tree of life. We create things we do not quite seek, then discover that they can organise our lives around themselves.

Welcome to capitalism, the unsaid religion of the twenty-first century. The comparison interested me because it concerned more than money. It concerned the arrangements through which we agreed that something deserved to exist, the things that received support and the questions that were easier to ask in favour of the arrangement than against it.

<a id="my-father-imagined-this-future-too"></a>

## My father imagined this future too

My dad grew up with images of flying cars, personal robots and space. When I imagined a technological future, I kept finding some of the same objects there.

Roughly half a century separated the two imaginations. The intervening work mattered: science, engineering, manufacturing, markets and the effort of making a possibility usable. But the recurrence still unsettled me. Why could a technology remain vivid as an image for so long before it became part of ordinary life?

Capitalism solves problems, not bare-metal mathematics. A scientific possibility does not organise its own adoption. Someone has to find a use, gather resources and make the arrangement work. That is one place where the delay enters. The science is not thereby finished, nor the engineering merely waiting for permission; the path from an idea to a shared way of living has several kinds of work inside it.

People, society and nature keep introducing the variable or two that the projection left out. We might succeed in pulling one over the chaos. I would hesitate to make a plan on the assumption that the odds were permanently in our favour.

<a id="what-receives-the-blessing"></a>

## What receives the blessing?

Capitalism’s latest rebound, in the metaphor I was carrying, was STEM. I was glad to see science and technology attract resources. We needed the tree to bear useful fruit. We had left an extraordinary mess on the planet, and hoping that somebody else would eventually clean it up was hardly an adequate response.

Yet that was also the question. Were we solving the problem, or delaying it long enough for a later generation to inherit the bill? A more sophisticated way to move the burden could look like progress from the place where the burden had disappeared.

Philosophy and ethics seemed less fortunate in the competition for resources. They could ask what prosperity meant and who paid for it, yet struggle to make their own work economically sustainable. The profitable imitation of moral concern could fare better than the patient inquiry. That contradiction made me angry.

Calling capitalism a religion was my way of asking which concerns it could hear. If an ethical question had no clear way to make money, did it still have a place at the table? If it challenged the table itself, who would pay for the chair?

<a id="silence-is-not-peace"></a>

## Silence is not peace

If capitalism could create a broad consensus around useful work, why not welcome it? Because consensus and peace were not always the same thing. People could be silent because they agreed, because they were exhausted or because the cost of speaking was too high.

The possibility of an all-seeing system concerned me for a similar reason. Convenience and coordination could become attractive ways to accept a power whose consequences were difficult to contest. Orwellian anxiety did not require every part of an Orwellian world to have arrived already.

We could continue fighting the wars of our forefathers in new institutions. We could also let corporations acquire a power over ordinary life that public debate struggled to understand. The question was not settled by declaring the institution benevolent or the technology inevitable. It returned to the room we had left for ethics, philosophy and dissent.

Education was central to that room. It produced the people who would lead every other institution, yet so often entered the discussion as an afterthought. I wanted education that was not captured by one partisan account, one soil or one school of thought. The work of examining an idea should not require swearing loyalty to it first.

<a id="becoming-what-i-condemn"></a>

## Becoming what I condemn

A conversation brought the question closer to home. We were talking about the things we did, the things we did not do and the things we once thought we would never do. The response to what had happened was the part we could work on most directly. For much of the rest, we could try; we could not command the outcome.

Attachment, fear and jealousy could gather around an identity until insecurity began making its decisions. A society could do this too. People defended inherited accounts of themselves, and the defence reproduced the behaviour they condemned in others.

So we went looking for cheat codes. Ways to notice the trap before the next response completed it. The difficulty was that even the effort to escape could feed on the same pride and insecurity. I could become very attached to the identity of somebody who had overcome attachment.

That is where the long-lost art of listening mattered. It asked me to let something enter that my identity had already decided was unwelcome. [Detachment and capitalism](https://getharsh.in/blog/holding-an-idea-without-becoming-it/) meet in that uncomfortable discovery: my response to the world is helping to make the world I will have to live in.

<a id="too-close-to-the-blue-pixel"></a>

## Too close to the blue pixel

We went looking, and I found myself thinking that we had been too close to the blue pixel to see the picture. An enormous conversation could be taking place while I argued with the one point nearest my eye.

The only way to be certain of the future is to create it. A surer way is to condemn it: respond from the same fear, reproduce the same arrangement, then recognise the thing I dreaded as something I helped make.

It was a provocation about participation, not a promise of certainty. What did I really know about the present? Enough, I hoped, to examine my response before sending another version of the same problem into the future.
