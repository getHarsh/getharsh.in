# When the compute question became larger

I began looking at AI infrastructure partly through the market: what was being built, where the constraints were, and what an investment in that change might really be exposed to. The question became harder to hold at that distance. To understand the market, I needed to understand the work the machines could complete. To understand that work, I had to look beyond the machines again.

An hourly GPU price is an appealing place to begin because it looks comparable. But a team does not want an inexpensive hour in isolation. It wants enough suitable capacity, at the right time, to carry a workload to a useful result. The memory, network, schedule and recovery path all become part of what it buys. [The completed job](https://rawthoughts.in/collections/useful-compute/a-gpu-price-is-not-a-job-cost/) (on RawThoughts) is a different economic object from the advertised hour.

That was the first change in my own inquiry. I had started by looking for an investment account of the AI-infrastructure market. Following procurement forced me to change the unit I was comparing. A reservation and an elastic allocation could quote the same kind of hour while asking the buyer to carry different kinds of risk. Availability, a deadline and the ability to recover unfinished work could reverse the apparent bargain. I became more interested in understanding that reversal than in producing another list of attractive providers.

<a id="the-machine-sits-inside-a-process"></a>

## The machine sits inside a process

Consider an illustrative team preparing a model evaluation for Friday. Capacity available on Saturday is not equivalent to capacity ready on Thursday, even if it is cheaper and faster once it starts. A wait changes the sequence of experiments, the time colleagues can use the result and the work that follows. Buying compute begins to look less like buying a commodity and more like arranging a process.

Inference adds a different pressure. A person waiting for an answer cares about how the service behaves under arriving demand. The same total throughput can accompany very different waiting times. [The service around generation](https://rawthoughts.in/blog/serving-tokens-is-a-service-problem/) (on RawThoughts) changes which capacity is useful, how much must be held ready and which apparent optimisation simply moves the cost to someone else.

These questions made engineering and economics harder to separate. A better execution arrangement can change the cost of an experiment. A better purchasing arrangement can make an otherwise impossible experiment available. Neither tells us whether the resulting capability will be adopted, who can afford it or how an organisation will change around it.

<a id="a-cheap-capability-can-meet-an-expensive-complement"></a>

## A cheap capability can meet an expensive complement

Suppose, as a thought experiment, that producing a competent document analysis becomes almost free. Its use can still depend on access to the document, a person able to judge an exception, a customer willing to change a process and an institution that accepts the result. The scarce part may have moved rather than disappeared.

At national scale, power, cooling, connectivity, access and the ability to turn output into productive work become harder to leave outside the account. That is the tension behind my [India infrastructure inquiry](https://rawthoughts.in/collections/ideas/india-ai/testing-indias-ai-crossing/from-posters-to-throughput/) (on RawThoughts). An announcement can describe an input while leaving the arrangement that makes it useful unresolved. Following the arrangement led me into questions about who captures value, which dependencies endure and what an expanding capability actually changes.

This is where the subject widened into [organisations and their complements](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/organisational-complements/) (on Post-AGI Economics). Lowering the cost of one task can change the division of work. It can also leave the existing organisation largely intact because another constraint still matters more. I became interested in telling those situations apart before declaring a new era on the strength of the component.

The infrastructure collection I was exploring did not stay one collection. Some questions became technical writing, others remained procurement and policy questions, and the larger economic ones grew into [Post-AGI Economics](https://post-agi-economics.org/) (on Post-AGI Economics). What had begun as a way to understand a market became a way to question the account of growth itself. I no longer wanted the engineering to be background colour for an investment thesis. I wanted to know which economic conclusion the engineering could actually support.

<a id="a-transition-has-more-than-one-clock"></a>

## A transition has more than one clock

The attraction of a single measure is real. A clock would offer a way to locate ourselves inside a change that otherwise feels too large to see. [One Yardstick](https://post-agi-economics.org/one-yardstick/) (on Post-AGI Economics) grew around that ambition. Working through the question made the differences between frontiers more important: a model can gain a capability before a firm has access, and a firm can gain access before it can rely on the capability in a valuable workflow.

The [time between those frontiers](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-clock-many-frontiers/) (on Post-AGI Economics) depends on what changes next. An improved model, a price change or a different institutional arrangement can alter the path itself. A clock has to account for those different transitions to make the larger change easier to examine. Which measures can do that remains part of the inquiry.

The economic consequence also depends on who owns the relevant assets and how income moves. Increased productive capacity is one part of that account; the ability to buy, access or share what it produces is another. [Productivity and purchasing power](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/productivity-and-purchasing-power/) (on Post-AGI Economics) meet in a real economy, but they do not become the same quantity because the technology connecting them is impressive.

The inquiry has taken me further from selecting winners and closer to understanding which question a model can answer. Sometimes a changing parameter is enough. Sometimes a different constraint becomes binding. Sometimes the actors or arrangements have changed so much that the old description leaves something essential out. That movement, from the price of a machine to the adequacy of an economic account, is what keeps this work open for me.
