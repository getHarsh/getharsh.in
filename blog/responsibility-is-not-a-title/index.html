Leading beyond the title

On this page

I have had amazing mentors, colleagues, friends and family, and one hell of a team. These are lessons from working with them and watching leadership emerge, including the times I got it wrong. I expect to learn better. But one thing has stayed with me: there is no nine-to-five switch for the responsibility. The people and consequences do not disappear when the meeting ends.

That is what makes leadership hard. I think of it as an art, with the balance to be found for yourself and your team. I want the work to succeed, and I want to be useful to the people doing it. Those wishes can pull in opposite directions when being useful starts to mean being needed for every decision. The harder achievement is helping the team grow beyond that dependence, then being glad that it has.

Whose ambition are we building?

A vision can be wonderfully clear inside my head and mean very little to the person I am asking to spend their life on it. What are they hoping to become? What are they giving up to be here? The company has objectives, but a team is made of people with purposes of their own. The interesting work is finding a direction they can recognise themselves in, not making my explanation so persuasive that their differences disappear.

At Smart Joules, my responsibilities grew from engineering into building and leading a product-development team. Hardware, software, research, testing, production and design could not each succeed in isolation. A technically correct component could still leave another team with an unusable system. People needed to understand the larger consequence of their decisions, not only the task assigned to them.

That kind of shared context takes time. It includes explaining why something matters, hearing where the plan does not fit and changing the division of work when the original assumptions were wrong. It cannot be replaced by a more inspiring slogan.

I think of the leader as the guardian of that vision, rather than its owner. The people doing the work give it life. Making the objectives clear is necessary management; leadership also asks whether the arrangements we built yesterday can carry today's problems, leaving the team room to build for tomorrow. If everyone is permanently fighting the present emergency, another ambitious roadmap has not given them a future.

Trust makes that possible. I have described leadership as an act of love, which may sound out of place beside a roadmap. But people are giving their time and hope to the work. A policy cannot substitute for the trust between them. Build it through honest conversations, with your guard down; if there is none, be willing to walk away. Empathy includes recognising that everyone brings experiences and boundaries of their own. It takes practice, and getting it wrong sometimes is a reason to keep learning, not to pretend the personal part can be removed.

Somebody still has to decide

There is always another projection we could prepare, another framework we could consult. Sometimes it would genuinely improve the decision. Sometimes it is how I postpone admitting that the available information will not make me certain. Waiting has consequences too. Experience can suggest a connection I cannot yet articulate, and it can make an old pattern feel applicable where it isn't. I need that intuition, but I also need to tell the team which part is a judgement and what would make us reconsider it. Otherwise my confidence becomes somebody else's unexplained risk.

Confidence that hides uncertainty borrows trust from the people who will bear the result. Endless hesitation asks those same people to carry the absence of a decision. The commitment needs to fit what the situation warrants now, with a way to learn quickly enough for the next decision to be better. Neither confidence nor hesitation removes that responsibility.

Agreement about a direction also differs from giving everyone the decision they wanted. A difficult choice can leave real disagreement. Buy-in means people understand why the choice was made, have had a chance to surface what it misses and can see how their work contributes from here. Seeking that commitment asks more of a leader than demanding consensus. It takes some distance from the plan to hear an objection without treating it as disloyalty.

When the result is embarrassing

Failures belong to the leader and success to the team: I hold that as an ethic about where to stand, rather than a literal account of who caused every event. People still have responsibilities, and causes still need examination. The leader's part is to remain present when the result exposes the direction or support they provided.

Success makes generosity easy. I can give the team credit and still enjoy how well the outcome reflects on me. Failure asks something less flattering: can somebody tell me that my direction was wrong without first taking care of my feelings? A requirement may have been unclear, the support insufficient or the promise unreasonable. If people have to protect my image before they can explain the failure, I have made the next decision harder for everyone.

Absorbing the blame helps little if everyone leaves reassured but carrying the same broken arrangement. We still need to understand what happened and who can change it. I want accepting and learning from failure to become inviting enough that people do it, including me. Run away from every failure and it begins making the next decision for you. Celebrating what we learned is part of making it possible to act again, rather than waiting until we can promise never to be wrong.

The team that no longer needs me for everything

Being needed can feel like proof that I am doing a good job. It is also an excellent way to become the bottleneck. Suppose somebody owns onboarding but has to ask me before changing a confusing step, speaking to support or investigating why customers leave. What have I actually given them? Perhaps the responsibility for reporting the problem, while keeping the useful decisions for myself. Delegation asks me to share the context and room to act, then stay available when the problem exceeds that arrangement.

Delegation becomes real when the person can carry the decision and understand where its limits are. Review can then examine the work instead of reclaiming every choice. Over time, the team should develop enough shared context and capability to do important things without the leader being the indispensable interpreter of everything. Making myself less necessary is part of building something that can continue.

There are people outside the mission, too

Leadership can involve difficult choices, personal sacrifices and work that will not fit a tidy routine. Family, support and the ability to ask for help belong in that picture; they are part of what lets someone carry the responsibility. That does not make exhaustion a measure of devotion. People have lives outside the mission, including the leader, and heroic availability can hide weak planning or inadequate capacity.

I have also worried about the difference between stopping work and recovering from it. For an entrepreneur, time away can leave the same demands running in the background. The question is whether that time actually restores the person who has to return, not merely whether the calendar calls it a break. I have no universal routine to offer; the distinction I want to keep visible is between being off the clock and being recharged.

Some commitments demand more than a comfortable routine; continuing others in the same way destroys the people making the work possible. I cannot settle that boundary for every situation. I want to know whether the sacrifice serves a purpose people understand, whether it is shared honestly and whether there is a path beyond the emergency. The leader may need support rather than another occasion to appear strong, and the people providing it bear consequences of their own.

After the title

Across employment, consulting and ventures, my formal role has changed. The recurring difficulty has been connecting a direction to the system, the team and the people affected by it. Leadership does not guarantee that an organisation survives, and a failed organisation does not make every contribution within it meaningless. The way people and work were treated determines what can still be learned and carried forward. A title can end while its consequences, relationships and example continue.

All of this can sound like the ramblings of a troubled mind. It is also why asking for help matters. Friends and family care for us when we are all out of love to give ourselves; they should not have to carry the consequences in silence.

When another opportunity arrives, the questions are personal again. Am I up for it? Will I make responsible and ethical choices when it gets too much? Am I willing to ask for help, rather than let the people closest to me carry a responsibility I refuse to name? Being bold is easier to admire from a distance than to live with every day.

It is a responsibility you earn, and you pay with scars. That is why I do not want exhaustion mistaken for having done it well. People often find enough time and money to do things twice. Would it not be better to take the time to do them properly the first time? There is no virtue in burning everybody out on the way. Straightforward, perhaps. Easy, no.
