When customers need less product

On this page

  4. Learning the system just to file the tax

Imagine buying software to make running your business easier, then discovering that your first task is to understand how the software company thinks. Its categories, configuration and navigation all make sense to somebody. Unfortunately, that somebody isn't you yet. The capability you need may be in there, surrounded by decisions you never expected to make.

Adding the missing feature gives a roadmap something obvious to say. Removing a burden asks a more awkward question: why did we make the customer carry it in the first place?

At Zenoti, I owned support, onboarding and taxation for the US and Canadian small-business market. Customer evidence led the work towards a simpler experience, rather than a better demonstration of the suite's breadth.

The work hiding in the support queue

I brought five years of support data together with product analytics, surveys and customer conversations. The breadth of the queue helped expose recurring friction; the conversations helped explain what customers were trying to accomplish when it appeared. The two forms of evidence could challenge each other. A frequently reported issue might look like a missing feature until the customer's account revealed a confusing decision or an external dependency.

“I cannot get started” brings several possible problems into the same queue. A customer may need an integration, be waiting on another provider or face incomplete imported records. They may also be able to complete the operation but struggle to find it. The diagnostic work connects the complaint to those causes: what exists, what is discoverable and which dependency prevents completion? Grouping tickets makes a recurring area visible; understanding the customer's task gives the roadmap a reason to change it.

Enough for this customer

The suite served a broader range of businesses than the SMB experience needed to expose. A capability useful elsewhere could impose configuration and navigation here without enough benefit. The segment's work gave us a way to decide which complexity was necessary, rather than treating the suite's full breadth as the natural starting point for every customer.

The evidence changed our roadmap in that direction: retain the requirements that mattered and reduce the surrounding burden. Buddy, the AI-first design system behind the revamped SMB experience, brought assistance and analytics into that narrower arrangement. The product reached customers, with self-onboarding and simpler taxation work among the changes I drove. The point of the revision was a more usable operation for this segment, rather than a smaller display of the same decisions.

This was not a general campaign for minimalism. A business with more complex operations can genuinely need a broader arrangement, and a smaller interface can conceal a necessary dependency. The product decision is about fit: the customer should not have to absorb the whole suite merely because the company knows how to build it.

Who is doing the onboarding now?

Turning an implementation team's work into a customer checklist can relocate the burden instead of removing it. The queue disappears from one part of the organisation, but the customer still has to interpret the configuration and coordinate its dependencies, now alone. Useful self-onboarding changes the work: avoid an unnecessary decision, explain the context of a necessary one and provide a route through an exception. The customer gains a way to begin, rather than an obligation to perform the implementation team's ideal procedure.

Payment onboarding makes the distinction concrete. The software can be ready while the provider still needs to complete its part. Data migration has a different dependency: a customer's email or phone may appear in several records, so a convenient match can merge people who should remain distinct. Preserving source customer IDs helps carry identity across the import; balances and appointments then need to remain attached to the right person. The customer encounters all of this as one business operation, even though the causes and remedies sit in different places.

Learning the system just to file the tax

Country-, case- and tax-code-specific work can ask a business operator to become an interpreter of the system's internal requirements. Streamlining that work meant reducing unnecessary translation between the customer's task and the product, so they could spend less time on manual filing and compliance inside it. Removing fields helped only if the remaining experience made the important decisions intelligible. A default that changes an obligation or payment still needs to be understood; an exception still needs a navigable path. Fewer screens can follow from that understanding, but they cannot supply it.

After the ticket closes

A resolved ticket can mean that a workaround was supplied, a configuration changed or a defect repaired. To understand the customer's experience, we need to follow what happened afterwards: could they finish the operation? A falling ticket count might reflect less friction or people giving up on asking. A faster setup among those who finish might coexist with more people abandoning it. Each measure sees one part of the work.

The useful comparison follows the customer through a defined task. How many begin, where do they stop, which exceptions require help and can they complete the work they came to do? The task, customer group and period give the measures a meaning. The revised experience reached customers, but understanding its effect requires following their work through those stages rather than treating the rollout itself as the result.

Knowing enough to ask less

Choosing what the customer should not have to understand is part of product judgment. Technical fluency exposes the dependencies; customer understanding helps determine which of them belong in the experience. The two forms of work meet when a simpler path keeps consequential choices intelligible while letting the person spend their attention on the business they came to run.

That lesson has stayed relevant as models make more responses possible. A more capable component does not require a more expansive product surface: the valuable operation may remain narrow, even when the model can talk about almost anything. Sometimes the stronger roadmap is the one that understands enough to ask less of the customer.
