Gravity was still acting on the magnet

On this page
  1. An extensive period of regression testing
  2. Bread on the table, questions on the way
  3. Two forces, one life

This reflection began in August 2024, looking back at my first decade of work. Its sense of standing at a turning point belongs to that moment.

I have been lucky in the people I have worked with. Academia, research, startups and communities put me beside minds that made me want to understand more. Who I would be working with often mattered more to me than what the work paid. That choice changed what I got to learn.

The work itself was rarely confined to the title. Cleaning the office floor, making PCBs, writing firmware, building a model, finding a vendor, supporting the customer, making a sale, raising money and helping a team grow could all belong to the same undertaking. A company starting from nothing does not politely wait for the correct department to exist.

I tried my hands at a great deal of it. As I thought you should. The first ten years seemed like a chance to discover what I was good at and what I was spectacularly capable of being bad at. Boy, did I use the opportunity.

An extensive period of regression testing

The experiments were not confined to work. I trained for MMA and entered tournaments, played piano, made art, hiked through different parts of India and spent time in monasteries. I read a lot, and bought more books than I could read. There were the pleasures and confusions of being in my twenties, too.

Eat, pray, love, if you want the familiar catalogue. I preferred to think of it as an extensive period of regression testing: eventually I might understand what all those Imtiaz Ali films had been going on about.

None of this fitted neatly around a career plan. It was the life in which the work was happening. A new interest could change the questions I brought back to an engineering problem. A difficult period could change how much I could carry, or what I thought was worth carrying. The person doing the job did not disappear when the job began.

Bread on the table, questions on the way

While I was discovering myself, I also had to put bread on the table. I joined Smart Joules as a founding engineer, moved into product development and support, worked as a product manager at Zenoti, and took part in ventures as an adviser, cofounder and consultant. The career journey follows those different responsibilities in their own settings.

Across them, artificial intelligence kept appearing. I had the good fortune to watch the distance between image-recognition work and language-model applications become part of ordinary product conversations. Being a nineties kid had already given me one version of that sensation: dial-up modems and floppy disks giving way to streaming on a phone. Suddenly the thing that had required effort and patience became something you expected to be there.

Timing mattered. I did not create the moment in which these possibilities arrived. I got to work while they were changing, and I got to learn from people trying to do something with them. I cannot tell that story honestly without saying how lucky I was.

The lessons also arrived outside the projects I was formally building. Presenting work, meeting someone after an event or sharing a few beers could open a conversation I would otherwise never have had. I learned from people just leaving college and from people who had spent more than forty years in industry. At barely thirty, I could learn from someone who had been doing the work longer than I had been alive.

Comparing those conversations, I kept coming back to leadership. Technical ability mattered, but so did how someone carried uncertainty, worked with other people and stayed with a problem. I was interested both in people who had made something work and people who had not made it work yet. That “yet” left room for the person to continue.

Two forces, one life

The analogy I liked was a pair of magnets dropped from a building. You might be studying gravitation, but electromagnetism has not agreed to stop operating for the convenience of your experiment. The magnets still attract or repel while they fall.

The professional and personal are like that. Calling one of them the subject of your attention does not suspend the other. The relationships, hopes and difficulties around the work still act on the person doing it. The work acts back.

In 2024, I felt myself reaching a turning point in both. I was imagining a life organised around more than my own next move. The decade behind me had felt like a sprint through experiences; I hoped the next one would become more of a marathon. I was projecting, of course. Time would have to tell me how well that description fitted.

But the possibility was already changing my priorities. I wanted to understand which parts of the first decade were experiments I had needed to try, which had become a practice worth sustaining, and what I would do differently if I began again.

That is where the next argument starts. The changing work of product management is one of the things those years taught me to care about. It comes from the engineering and the customers, certainly. It also comes from the people, the interests outside work and the life in which all of it had to make sense.
