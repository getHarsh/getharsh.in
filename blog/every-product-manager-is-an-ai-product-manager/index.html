Every digital product manager must become an AI product manager

The argument began in September 2024 as the second part of a reflection on building products. Its predictions describe the direction I was arguing for then; the model examples belong to that period.

A product manager who understands the customer but cannot follow what the technology makes possible is increasingly working with half the picture. The reverse is just as limiting. Knowing a model's architecture does not tell us why somebody would return to the product tomorrow.

My argument is that every digital product manager now needs to become an AI product manager. Not because every product needs a chatbot, but because AI can change the behaviour the product serves, the work behind it and the business that sustains it. Treating the subject as a feature that another team can add later leaves those choices to chance.

From flipping pages to scrolling feeds

Remember when the programme schedule told us when to watch, and television ratings helped decide which programmes could continue? Compare that with opening YouTube or Netflix and finding a selection arranged for you. The viewer's choices, the distribution system and the business's measures have all moved.

The newspaper-to-feed transition makes the change almost comically physical. We moved from turning pages to scrolling down a screen. A thumb's new habit rested on touchscreens, networks, ranking systems, recommendation research and years of adoption. It was not just a designer choosing a different direction for the same page.

Personalised feeds made relevance and engagement part of a continuing interaction. Product work had to translate frustrations with static or irrelevant content into questions that engineers and researchers could address. Which content should appear, in what order, for whom, and what would count as a better result?

The shift was visible in use. In a survey conducted in 2020 and published in January 2021, Pew Research Center found that 86% of US adults got news from digital devices at least sometimes, including 60% who did so often. Those are distinct measures, not a single interchangeable adoption percentage.

Streaming had grown too, without already becoming all television. Nielsen's August 2020 account put streaming at a quarter of television viewing in OTT-capable homes; Netflix and YouTube accounted for 34% and 20% of that streaming time. The denominator matters. The change in the product opportunity does not need an inflated account of how complete the transition was.

The research changes what can be built

My own route through AI began closer to image processing and embedded systems than to a conversational window. Looking across the field, the sequence from LeNet's digit recognition to AlexNet, BERT and large language models shows how research changes the kinds of product questions we can ask.

AlexNet's 2012 paper described a convolutional network with 60 million parameters and an efficient GPU implementation. BERT, introduced in 2018, made bidirectional language representations useful across tasks such as question answering and language inference. GPT-3 in 2020 explored a 175-billion-parameter model whose tasks could be specified through instructions and examples in text. The first LLaMA family in 2023 ranged from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters and made a different scale-and-efficiency comparison possible.

Those are changes in architecture, training, data and use, not a league table in which more parameters automatically means a better product. By September 2024, the arrival of reasoning-oriented models was pushing my expectations further. I saw an expanding space of tasks that software might handle. A result on a particular benchmark still cannot establish that a model reasons better than a human at everything, or that its result can be trusted in the workflow we are building.

The product manager needs enough understanding to ask where the advance matters. Can the user begin with an intention instead of a category? Can a worker examine alternatives that were previously too expensive to prepare? Does a smaller specialised model make a useful operation economical? A technical change becomes a product opportunity when we can connect it to somebody's work.

The iPhone was a product argument

The iPhone is useful to me as an example of empathy meeting technical construction. The 2007 introduction brought phone, media and internet functions together around a large multi-touch interface. It changed what the screen and software could ask of the user's fingers. The opportunity was larger than making a physical keyboard slightly better.

That does not require pretending Apple invented every touchscreen or that the entire ecosystem appeared on the first day. The App Store followed in 2008. Hardware, software, developer tools and distribution became parts of a larger arrangement through which other people could build.

What interests me is the work concealed by the experience. Research, interface design, patents and engineering have to meet before a complicated system can feel natural. The customer does not need a tour of each component to benefit from it. Product judgement has to understand enough of those components to make the natural experience possible.

The same thought appears in Nike's appeal to the person becoming an athlete. The material and construction matter because of the experience they enable. The product does not become meaningful merely by reciting its technical properties.

What new habits might AI make possible?

Personalisation is one direction. In its 2018 survey, Accenture reported that 91% of respondents were more likely to shop with brands that recognised them and supplied relevant recommendations. That is a reported preference in a particular survey, not a guarantee that every attempt at prediction will be welcomed.

The ambition I was describing goes beyond ordering a familiar catalogue. A service might anticipate the next useful step, prepare a relevant option or carry the context of an unfinished task across devices. Imagine beginning on a phone and continuing in a car or at home without reconstructing the intention each time. That is a product hypothesis about continuity; making it useful also requires the person to understand and control what is carried across those settings.

Another direction is less visible technology. Think of a service such as Swiggy meeting the operating system through an action or notification instead of requiring every interaction to begin with a tour of its app. AI could make more capabilities available where the need arises. The further possibility is an “intelligence store”: capabilities that other products can call and compose, rather than a separate app for every narrow interaction.

These possibilities ask product managers to guide research towards behaviour worth enabling. Seamlessness is not a substitute for that judgement. The fact that two systems can connect does not establish that a person wants them connected, or that the resulting service helps.

Think through the system, not only the feature

In an enterprise product, a model may connect processes that already have their own rules, records and users. It could help interpret a request, prepare an analysis or move information between teams. The apparent intelligence sits inside a system that still needs consistent identities, compatible versions, current data and a way to handle exceptions.

This is why I want product managers to practise systems thinking. The engineering remains engineering; a product manager does not become its substitute. But a roadmap needs to recognise dependencies instead of treating each visible feature as an independent promise.

A framework such as the V-model offers one useful habit: connect a requirement to how it will be checked, from the whole system down to the parts. For an AI-assisted operation, that means deciding what the user must be able to complete and what evidence would show that the model and surrounding software support it.

The teams also need a shared vocabulary. We already translate a business concern such as daily active users into engineering questions about traffic, capacity and latency. Model performance needs a similarly explicit relationship to product objectives. Accuracy on which cases? A response time measured from which event? An improvement for which customer task? If research, engineering and the business each mean something different by “better,” the roadmap can advance while the product stands still.

Human review must have something to review

Control belongs in the experience. A person may need to inspect a recommendation, correct an assumption, stop an action or recover from a bad result. Validation and rollback cannot remain reassuring words attached after the main flow has been designed.

Nor does a model's fluent explanation necessarily reveal how it reached the answer. Useful transparency gives the reader the evidence, assumptions and proposed consequence they can actually examine. In the bill-dispute example, a person needs to see which bill, which amount and why a correction is proposed. A generic confirmation button asks for trust without providing the basis for it.

The appropriate review depends on the decision. A suggestion and an irreversible change need different controls. Product management has to make that distinction part of the operation, working with the people responsible for its risks and recovery.

A roadmap has to outlive the demonstration

The first useful version should leave room for the system to grow. That means understanding which capabilities can be composed, which assumptions are tied to one model and what later integrations would need. The App Store analogy matters here because the larger opportunity came from an environment in which other products could operate.

It does not mean predicting every future model. A modular design gives us places to learn and replace something without pretending the surrounding workflow has no dependencies. We still need to know what a change does to the user's experience, cost and ability to recover.

Feedback supplies one route to improvement. Reinforcement learning from human feedback is one technique; collecting reactions is not itself a training process. The product has to make the feedback interpretable, decide what it should influence, and compare a proposed change before releasing it. A complaint could point to a model weakness, a wrong source, a confusing interface or a business rule the user dislikes. Training on all four as though they were the same problem would hide the work we needed to do.

This is the move towards research management that I meant. Product managers need to help researchers choose questions tied to real customer difficulties, while keeping room for work whose value is not yet a feature on next month's plan. The conversation goes both ways: research can reveal a possibility the original roadmap did not know to ask for.

The business result is part of the product

Retention, efficiency and operating cost give the technical work an economic purpose. In their 2015 account of Netflix's recommender system, Carlos Gomez-Uribe and Neil Hunt estimated that personalisation and recommendations together saved more than a billion dollars a year, through effects including reduced churn. That was their company estimate for the combined system, not the value of one algorithm or a return another business can assume.

The relationship is the useful lesson. A recommendation matters because it helps a member find something worth watching and return to the service. A developer tool matters because it helps the developer complete work. A cheaper model call is not an operating saving if it creates enough retries and support to outweigh it.

Consumer empathy and technical fluency meet at that level. One helps us understand the work somebody values. The other helps us shape a system that can deliver it. Research expands what might be possible; engineering and the business have to make the result usable and sustainable.

That is why I do not think an “AI-powered” label is an adequate ambition for a product manager. The work is to help shape new behaviour, connect the capability to a real outcome and keep the technology from becoming the customer's burden. Don't be a generic “AI-powered” product manager. ;)
