Dumb comedy: existentialism for the twenty-first century

On this page
  1. New year, same difficult whys
  2. Who makes the monsters?
  3. Enjoy the little things
  4. What would Phil Dunphy do?

Written at the end of 2019, with a new decade approaching.

Yup. That is exactly what it sounds like. I root for Ross. I root for JD. I root for Marshall, Leonard and Phil. Different people in different sitcoms, yet there is something familiar about their predicament: the outlier, the geek, the person everybody is comfortable laughing at. The adorable ones.

Their plight makes me think about education and its place in society. Its fall feels like our biggest unpatched bug. We leave room for a character to be foolish, persistent and oddly perceptive on television. Around our water coolers and balconies, we are less patient with somebody whose question does not fit the conversation. We discuss proposals, law, ethics, experience and philosophy as though the boundaries between them were obvious. Meanwhile, the supposedly dumb ones keep trying to clear the haze for whoever comes next.

Why am I talking about this? Because it may be one of the few things on which I can agree with a lot of people. Stretch most other conversations over a decade and they start to resemble horror stories.

New year, same difficult whys

The point of “New Year, New Me,” surely, is to look at what we have created before deciding what we will create next. Get your priorities straight, as the old man would put it. Have your whys in order.

This little nugget comes from a Certified Scrum Master. If you have no idea what that means, you lucky bastard. In a nutshell: consensus is tricky. 😉

One of my largest lessons of the decade was that a teacher need not have the form I expected. There are sayings about the student becoming ready and the teacher appearing. A sitcom could supply a small version of that encounter. It could show me something I had become too serious to notice in myself.

And if you don’t believe the horror-story bit, look around at what is happening to democracies. Not just between people and governments, but between corporations and governments, and corporations and people. Don’t even get me started on the last one. I agreed with Socrates’ criticism of democracy: he had good reasons to distrust our collective judgement. That unpatched bug in education kept making the old fellow look right.

Who makes the monsters?

But there is a ray of hope too. The war on ego and ignorance is on all fronts; the hardest is often the one I have with myself. Conflict can produce art. Artists exploring this existence are leading the change, reaching millions in ways more profound than institutional policy. People are finding art in all sorts of things.

We have been so busy looking for monsters and heroes in our electronics-powered algebraic intelligence (God damn Electricity as a Service) that we forget how we create both every day. It is a privilege to remain insulated from what people do to other people and their data, then find worse monsters and better heroes in a world made from pixels. We pay for that world every month. The same day, the same time. Exercising our privilege when it matters is a choice too.

The privilege is not itself the whole moral question. What do I do with it? When does it let me avoid something I should face, and when does it give me enough room to continue?

Isn’t that what war comes down to: the ruthless calculus of survival and submission? We work to feed ourselves and our families, to have an occasional drink and a little room for pleasure. Then comes the old question: who saves the hero?

Enjoy the little things

Perhaps this is where the memes help. Zombieland gives me rule 32: “Enjoy the little things.” I keep returning to it.

Dumb comedy is not necessarily easy. Sometimes it performs the hardest job: giving a person a small reprieve without demanding that they solve existence first. A joke lets me live and fight another day for whatever the larger work was about. It need not pretend that the work has gone away.

History, I kept thinking, is written by the victors. What we do tomorrow will be part of it the day after. A little reprieve lets us return to that struggle instead of being swallowed by it. You can be a monster or a hero, both or neither. Just don’t be an asshole to the ones who choose differently, and we can sit back to watch this little documentary play out a decade from now.

Some rules are made of glass, not rubber. Be careful when you break them. A reminder to enjoy a small thing is one I would rather keep.

What would Phil Dunphy do?

For me, entertainment is supposed to entertain. Sometimes I want it to take me away from the reality I took a break from, rather than return that reality in a more elaborate costume. The heist of a heist of a heist can wait.

When I feel the familiar anxiety gathering, I take a breath and ask myself what Phil Dunphy would do. I learn from the master, then return to the what and the how.

Pro tip from the holiday version of me: leave the whys to the holidays. And remember, nothing good ever happens after 2 a.m. If you have a PhD in sitcoms, be kind and pass the references along.

I had finally got my hands on Jean Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation. I planned to go through it over the holidays, hoping to clear some of the haze. Also, what’s your take on Rick and Morty and existentialism?
